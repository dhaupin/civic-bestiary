# The Civic Bestiary

A field guide to the characters of the body politic: portmanteaus for performative patriotism, bad-faith debate, hypocrisy, and algorithmic radicalization - plus the documented fallacies they commit.

**[Read the field guide](https://codex.creadev.org)** - published by [Creadev](https://creadev.org)

## Preface

This is a bestiary, not a dictionary. The characters in it are real, common, and close by - the flag hoodie in the school-board queue, the centrist with one newsletter, the commentator monetizing your uncle’s anger. Each species is named the way a naturalist would name it: by the trait that makes it unmistakable, and by the psy-loop it runs.

Two rules keep the guide from collapsing into a list of insults. The species is a pattern, not a person - a costume that wears people, not a kind of human, and one you can catch yourself in. And every entry describes a mechanism you could point at in a transcript, a rally, or a comment thread, so the name stays a tool for recognition rather than a way to end an argument.

The coinages are portmanteaus on purpose: legible on first contact, slightly ridiculous on second thought. Some of these characters are trolls in the old sense, things that live under bridges and demand a toll; some are trolls in the newer one. Both are field-observed here. That is roughly the right posture for the subject.

The fallacies work the other way round. They are not coinages and are not ours: they are documented errors in reasoning, named by logicians and studied by psychologists, and every one of them links to its source. Each chapter ends with the documented errors its own characters commit, so the joke arrives with its receipt attached. The definitions here are written in this guide's voice, but nothing in them is invented - if a claim about a fallacy cannot be traced to the literature, it does not go in the record. The errors that turn up in every chapter and belong to none get a chapter of their own, because a fallacy no character owns is one the whole room has stopped noticing - that is chapter V, and it is the one you will meet most often. The coinages are the joke; the fallacies are the part you can cite.

## I. Performative Identity & Weaponized Liberty

### 1. Playtriot
*(noun)* `/ˈpleɪ.tri.ət/` - *species*  
*Blend of "play" + "patriot"*

**Occasion:** A question about policy that has just been answered with a question about loyalty.  
**Call:** "Do you support the troops?"  
**Tell:** Flag decal on a leased vehicle · Quotes a founder, never a statute · Knows the anthem's tune, not its third verse  
**Counter:** Answer the flag question, then ask the policy question again. Patriotism is not a reply, and it does not survive being asked what it costs.  

1. The pattern of substituting the symbols of citizenship for its obligations: the flag worn, the anthem invoked, the ballot skipped, the jury excused.
2. It works because symbols are cheap and legible while obligations are slow and unphotogenic, so the display can carry a loyalty the conduct would otherwise have to earn.

* **Example:** *"He wrapped himself in the flag at every rally, yet his refusal to respect basic civic responsibilities showed he was more of a **playtriot** than a true patriot."*
* **Easily confused with:** *LARPtarian* - Both dress the part; the playtriot buys the flag, the LARPtarian buys the surplus rifle.

### 2. Freetard
*(noun)* `/ˈfriː.tɑːrd/` - *species*  
*Blend of "free" + "retard" [verb: to delay, hinder, or impede progress]*

**Occasion:** A rule that applies to everyone, and a person who has just found out it applies to them.  
**Call:** "It's a free country."  
**Tell:** Confuses a right with an exemption · Cites freedom, never a clause · Loudest where the line is longest  
**Counter:** Ask which clause, and what it says. A right that cannot be quoted is a preference with a flag on it.  

1. The pattern of invoking a right without its text: liberty asserted as a mood, then applied to a rule the speaker has not read.
2. It works because rights are real and specific, so a claim that sounds like one is hard to refuse in public - and the specificity only appears when somebody asks which clause.

* **Etymology & Usage Note:** Derived from the traditional verb *retard* (as in *fire-retardant*), signifying an understanding of freedom actively slowed or stunted by external agendas. Because the suffix *-tard* is commonly used online as an ableist slur, readers unfamiliar with this specific root framing may still interpret the word as derogatory without context.
* **Example:** *"Because his view of liberty was completely **retarded** by partisan talking points, the **freetard** argued that endangering his neighbors was his constitutional right."*
* **Easily confused with:** *Critizen* - The freetard wants no rules; the critizen insists there already are none that apply to him.
* **Commits:** [*Appeal to nature*](#23-appeal-to-nature), [*Two wrongs make a right*](#90-two-wrongs-make-a-right)
* **Related Terms:** [*LARPtarian*](#3-larptarian), [*Playtriot*](#1-playtriot)

### 3. LARPtarian
*(noun)* `/ˈlɑːrpˌtɛər.i.ən/` - *species*  
*Blend of "LARP" [Live Action Role-Play] + "libertarian"*

**Occasion:** A conversation about self-reliance, held by someone with a mortgage and a subscription list.  
**Call:** "I could live off the grid tomorrow."  
**Tell:** Solar panels on a financed roof · Owns a crossbow, no arrows · Files an HOA complaint about a fence  
**Counter:** Ask what happens on the third day. Preparedness is a supply chain, and the fantasy usually ends where the delivery does.  

1. The pattern of performing self-reliance while living on the thing being denounced: the rebel with the mortgage, the survivalist with the subscription.
2. It works because the costume is legible and the dependency is invisible, and nobody audits the third day of a fantasy.

* **Example:** *"Posting about 'living off the grid' from a climate-controlled suburb with municipal water and paved roads is peak **LARPtarian** behavior."*
* **Easily confused with:** *Playtriot* - The LARPtarian can name the rifle's caliber; the playtriot can only name the flag's manufacturer.

### 4. Critizen
*(noun)* `/ˈkrɪt.ɪ.zən/` - *species*  
*Blend of "critic" + "citizen" - the citizen whose critique of the rules exempts them from the rules*

**Occasion:** An obligation with a form attached, a deadline behind it, and a queue that has already started moving.  
**Call:** "That doesn't apply to me."  
**Tell:** Names the defect in the rule, never the rule · Hand-lettered plate, unsigned form, unworn lanyard · Owes back taxes on principle  
**Counter:** Ask for the citation, then read it aloud. Every defect this species alleges has an address, and the claim rarely survives being read in the room where it was made.  

1. The pattern of treating a shared obligation as something that binds other people: the tax refused on a theory, the summons answered with a citation, the permit argued instead of filed.
2. It works because a rule is cheaper to dispute than to obey - the objection costs a sentence and the deadline costs a day - and a defect, once named, sounds like diligence rather than exemption.

* **Former name:** Formerly **Sovereign Shillizen**, a name that aimed at the shill and missed the wider species: the shillizen is one costume the critizen wears, and not the most common one.
* **Example:** *"Pulled over on plates he had printed himself, the **critizen** explained that he was travelling rather than driving, and that the officer was a corporation."*
* **Easily confused with:** *Freetard* - The critizen has a theory about why the rule is void; the freetard has a feeling that it should be.
* **Commits:** [*Equivocation*](#146-equivocation), [*Argument from ignorance*](#54-argument-from-ignorance), [*I'm entitled to my opinion*](#26-im-entitled-to-my-opinion), [*Just-world hypothesis*](#159-just-world-hypothesis)

### 5. Sabotriot
*(noun)* `/ˈsæb.ə.tri.ət/` - *species*  
*Blend of "saboteur" + "patriot"*

**Occasion:** A service that has just closed, and a ballot that closed it.  
**Call:** "I voted for the guy who'll cut it."  
**Tell:** Votes against the subsidy, keeps the check · Waves the flag at the shuttered plant · Blames the other party for the closure  
**Counter:** Put the vote next to the closure. This is not a private hypocrisy; it is a public choice with a public receipt.  

1. The pattern of voting against the service you use: the library closed by the ballot, the clinic defunded by the same hand that needed it.
2. It works because a vote is private and a closure is public, and the distance between them is where the explanation usually stops.

* **Example:** *"Cheerfully voting to defund his own town's hospital and public schools just to spite people in another zip code makes him a textbook **sabotriot**."*
* **Easily confused with:** *Playtriot* - A playtriot performs the loyalty; a sabotriot votes it into your hospital's closure.

### 6. Patriotainment
*(noun)* `/ˈpeɪ.tri.ə.ˌteɪn.mənt/` - *habitat*  
*Blend of "patriot" + "entertainment"*

**Carriers:** Cable primetime, streaming specials, rally tours, and the merch drop that follows each one.  
**Economics:** Ad rates keyed to outrage, an eighty-dollar hoodie, and a subscription tier for the uncut version.  
**Signature:** A countdown clock over a battlefield, and a sponsor read that starts before the anthem finishes.  
**Counter:** Change the payment. A subscription, a public broadcaster, or one night without the channel - the format follows the revenue, and nothing else moves it.  

1. The commercial apparatus that converts national identity into subscription content - merch lines, streaming specials, rally tours - where loving the country is something you buy a ticket to watch rather than something you owe the people living in it.
2. A mode of civic life in which the spectacle of patriotism substitutes entirely for its obligations, so that cheering loudest counts as service and noticing anything else counts as betrayal.

* **Example:** *"Eighty-dollar hoodies, four-hundred-dollar seats, and a two-hour set about how the country is under attack - that isn't a movement, that's **patriotainment** at arena scale."*

### 7. Patriotainer
*(noun)* `/ˈpeɪ.tri.ə.ˌteɪ.nər/` - *species*  
*Back-formation from "patriotainment": the performer the format cannot run without*

**Occasion:** A commercial break that needs a grievance to sell the next segment.  
**Call:** "Folks, they don't want you to know this."  
**Tell:** Every segment ends on a sponsor read · Owns the flag as a prop and licenses the footage · Says "the country" when meaning "my audience" · No position survives a ratings dip  
**Counter:** Ask what was withheld and who was paid. The audience is the product, and the "they" is usually the sponsor.  

1. The pattern of converting national identity into inventory: the flag as a prop, the outrage as a slot, the certainty as a requirement of the format.
2. It works because the segment is paid for by attention, and a conclusion is more bookable than a question - so being right becomes a liability no sponsor will underwrite.

* **Example:** *"He spent one segment mourning the republic and the next selling the supplement that would save it - a **patriotainer** to the last ad break."*
* **Easily confused with:** *Playtriot* - The playtriot performs for free and believes it; the patriotainer performs on contract and invoices for it.
* **Easily confused with:** *Patriotainment* - The format is the show; the patriotainer is the person the show is built around - and the one who gets replaced when the timeslot moves.

### 8. Filibluster
*(noun / verb)* `/ˈfɪl.ɪˌblʌs.tər/` - *ability*  
*Blend of "filibuster" + "bluster"*

**Effect:** Converts a minority into a veto by outlasting the clock, without ever having to win the argument.  
**Available to:** Any member of a chamber that still keeps a speaking clock, a quorum, and a camera.  
**Counter:** Cloture, a quorum that stays awake, or the nuclear option - each of which spends the institution to buy back the hour.  

1. The procedural power of a legislative minority to stop the body from acting by refusing to stop talking - a capability of the seat rather than the person, available to anyone willing to read the phone book into the record.
2. It works because the rules protect the speaker, not the subject. The longer a speech runs, the more the room's patience - and not its argument - becomes the thing under negotiation, so the win goes to whoever has least to lose by staying.

* **Example:** *"He read the bill's table of contents, then the Bible, then his own memoir; by hour nine the compromise was dead and the cameras had gone home - a textbook **filibluster**."*
* **Easily confused with:** *Omnibust* - The filibluster is performed aloud, for the clock; the omnibust is performed at 3 a.m., for the printer.
* **Commits:** [*Ad nauseam*](#132-ad-nauseam), [*Gish gallop*](#154-gish-gallop)
* **Related Terms:** [*Dramagogue*](#97-dramagogue), [*Patriotainer*](#7-patriotainer)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Filibuster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filibuster)

*Blend of "gerrymander" + "algorithm"*

**Effect:** Draws the district you are going to win, at machine speed, before anyone has voted.  
**Available to:** State legislatures with a census, a map, and a data vendor on retainer.  
**Counter:** An independent commission with a drawing mandate, or a court willing to read the code.  

1. The capability of a legislature to choose its voters instead of the reverse - now run by software that tests ten thousand maps overnight against turnout history and keeps the one most likely to survive a court.
2. It works because a district line is not an argument, so it never has to be defended as one. By the time anyone can prove the intent, the map has already elected three cycles of the people who drew it.

* **Example:** *"The county split cleanly down a cul-de-sac, and the incumbent's house landed in a district with none of his old voters - the **gerrythm** had run overnight and nobody had seen the code."*
* **Easily confused with:** *Filibluster* - The filibluster wins the hour; the gerrythm wins the decade.

### 10. Omnibust
*(noun)* `/ˈɒm.nɪˌbʌst/` - *ability*  
*Blend of "omnibus" + "bust"*

**Effect:** Passes a thousand pages nobody has read by making the reading of them the thing that keeps the lights off.  
**Available to:** Appropriators, at three in the morning, with a shutdown clock on the wall.  
**Counter:** A single-subject rule, a mandatory reading period, or a clerk with stamina and a public log.

### 11. Honeymoan
*(noun)* `/ˈhʌn.iˌməʊn/` - *buff*

**Tell:** "Give him a chance" from someone who gave the last one four years of scrutiny and no chance at all.

### 12. Pocketveto
*(noun / verb)* `/ˈpɒk.ɪtˌviː.təʊ/` - *ability*  
*Blend of "pocket" + "veto", from the constitutional pocket veto*

**Effect:** Kills a bill by doing nothing, inside a ten-day window the chamber is not allowed to watch.  
**Available to:** A president, a bill passed near the end of a session, and a Congress that has gone home.  
**Counter:** Stay in session. A pro forma gavel every few days keeps the ten-day clock running and forces the objection into writing.  

1. The constitutional power to let a bill die unsigned: the president neither signs nor returns it, Congress adjourns within ten days, and the bill is simply not law - no message, no veto to override, no vote to defend.
2. It works because absence is not an act. A regular veto is a public document that invites a two-thirds fight; a pocket veto is a calendar, and there is nobody to argue with. The clause was written for a president travelling by horse, and it still works because Congress still goes home.

* **Example:** *"The bill passed in December, the president went to Camp David, and the **pocketveto** did what a veto message could not - it ended the argument without leaving a record to run against."*

### 13. Nightdocket

**Available to:** An appellate court with an emergency application, a filing deadline, and no argument on the calendar.

### 16. Cloture
*(noun)* `/ˈkləʊ.tʃə/` - *ability*  
*English: from the French clôture, the closing - the motion that ends debate.*

**Effect:** Ends a debate by arithmetic, at a price the majority has to pay out of its own calendar.  
**Available to:** Sixty senators, or a majority willing to spend the days it takes to get to sixty.  
**Counter:** Count the votes before you need them. Cloture is cheap for a majority that already has the number, and a week it cannot spare for one that does not.  

1. The motion that closes debate: a supermajority votes to stop talking and the chamber moves to the question - the procedural exit from a filibuster, priced in session days rather than in votes.
2. It works because the price is time, and time is the one thing a majority cannot borrow. The two-step - cloture on the motion to proceed, then cloture on the bill - means a determined minority is charged twice for the same hour.

* **Example:** *"They had the votes for the bill and not the votes for **cloture**, so the week went to the calendar and the bill went to the next session."*

### 17. Discharge petition

**Available to:** A majority of the House - 218 signatures - and a bill that a chair has decided to bury.  
**Counter:** Keep the signatures public and the bill moving. A petition is expensive to sign and cheap to schedule, so the cheapest counter is a hearing.

### 18. Vague promises

### 19. Citizens United
*(noun)* `/ˌsɪt.ɪ.zənz juːˈnaɪ.tɪd/` - *ability*  
*Named for the 2010 decision that made independent political spending a form of speech, and the spender a kind of speaker.*

**Effect:** The capability of money to speak in the first person: the expenditure is protected as speech, so the corporation gains the citizen's voice without taking on the citizen's obligations.  
**Available to:** Any incorporated body with a treasury - a company, a union, a nonprofit, or a shell with a mailbox and a lawyer.  
**Counter:** Disclosure before the vote rather than after it. An advertisement that has to name its funders loses the anonymity the doctrine depends on.  

1. The doctrine that spending is speech: the ad is the argument, the treasury is the lung, and a limit on either is a limit on the speaker.
2. It works because the first amendment was written for people and the corporation learned to stand where the person stood. What the citizen has is a voice and a vote; what the doctrine grants is the voice, at any volume, without the vote's obligation to answer for it.

* **Example:** *"**Citizens United** means we are just exercising our speech" - said by a body that has never been to a town meeting and cannot be summoned to one.*

### 20. Slippery slope
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*The image is physical: one step onto the incline, and gravity is left to finish the argument.*

**Family:** Informal · Presumption  
**Committed when:** A modest change is proposed that the speaker opposes.  
**The tell:** The sentence jumps from a small rule to a catastrophe with no institution in between.  
**Counter:** Ask what stops the slide at each step. If the answer is nothing, ask why the previous steps never slid.  

1. Claiming that a small first step will inevitably lead to an extreme outcome, without showing the mechanism that carries it there.
2. It works because nobody can audit a chain of consequences they have not seen. Each link sounds plausible alone; the whole chain is narrated rather than argued.

* **Example:** *"First they ban the plastic bag, then they ban the car."* Between the two there are four legislatures, a court, and an election, none of which appears in the sentence.
* **Easily confused with:** *False dilemma* - The slope hides a chain of steps; the dilemma hides the options that are not steps.
* **Related Terms:** [*Post hoc ergo propter hoc*](#91-post-hoc-ergo-propter-hoc), [*Nirvana fallacy*](#63-nirvana-fallacy)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Slippery slope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slippery_slope)

### 21. No true Scotsman
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From Antony Flew's example: no Scotsman puts sugar on his porridge - and any Scot who does is reclassified.*

**Family:** Informal · Faulty generalization  
**Committed when:** A counterexample has just been produced against a generalisation about a group.  
**The tell:** The group acquires a new membership test, applied only to the case at hand.  
**Counter:** Fix the definition before the counterexamples arrive, and ask which rule excluded this one and why that rule was not mentioned earlier.  

1. Protecting a generalisation from counterexamples by redefining the category until the counterexamples fall outside it.
2. It works because definitions are genuinely negotiable. The sentence sounds like a clarification, and a clarification is much harder to argue with than a false claim.

* **Example:** *"No real conservative would vote for that."* The vote happened; the sentence has quietly moved the boundary so it cannot count against the brand.
* **Easily confused with:** *Straw man* - The straw man weakens the opponent's claim; the Scotsman strengthens the speaker's, by shrinking the group until the counterexample is expelled.
* **Related Terms:** [*Moving the goalposts*](#127-moving-the-goalposts), [*Nut-picking*](#59-nut-picking)

### 22. Appeal to tradition
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: argumentum ad antiquitatem - the old is treated as the proven.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A practice is being questioned and its defenders have no account of why it works.  
**The tell:** The argument's only support is that the practice has always been there.  
**Counter:** Ask what the practice does now. Duration is a reason to be careful, not a reason to stop asking.  

1. Treating a practice as correct because it is old, without showing that age is evidence of anything.
2. It works because inherited arrangements carry the weight of everyone who lived with them. Age is genuine information about endurance; it is not information about fairness or function.

* **Example:** *"We have always opened with a prayer."* The question was whether the chamber should, and always is not an answer to should.
* **Easily confused with:** *Appeal to nature* - Tradition says it is old, nature says it is natural; both replace a reason with a pedigree.

### 23. Appeal to nature
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: argumentum ad naturam - the natural is treated as the good, as though the word carried a verdict.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A product, practice, or hierarchy needs a justification.  
**The tell:** The justification is a claim about what is natural, with no account of why natural is good.  
**Counter:** Ask which part of nature is the model. Nature also contains arsenic, parasites, and every hierarchy that ever existed.  

1. Concluding that something is right or healthy because it is natural, or wrong because it is artificial.
2. It works because "natural" smuggles in a standard it never defends. Nature is a description; the sentence spends it as an endorsement.

* **Example:** *"It is natural for people to sort themselves by tribe."* It is also natural to die of an infected cut, and we stopped doing that on purpose.
* **Easily confused with:** *Appeal to tradition* - Tradition cites age, nature cites origin; both answer a question about value with a question about ancestry.
* **Related Terms:** [*Hasty generalization*](#142-hasty-generalization), [*Argumentum ad populum*](#150-argumentum-ad-populum)

### 24. Proof by assertion
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From mathematics, where a proof is a demonstration - and this is the counterfeit: the conclusion, repeated.*

**Family:** Informal · Presumption  
**Committed when:** The claim cannot be supported and must not be conceded.  
**The tell:** The same sentence returns, louder, with no new reason attached.  
**Counter:** Ask for the reason once, then note that none has arrived. Repetition is not a premise, and saying so out loud is usually enough.  

1. Repeating a claim with confidence as though repetition were support, and treating objection as noise.
2. It works because certainty is contagious. An audience without the means to check infers from composure that there is something to check against.

* **Example:** *"It is the worst deal in history. The worst deal. Everybody knows it is the worst deal."* Four sentences, one assertion, zero evidence.
* **Easily confused with:** *Circular reasoning* - The assertion adds no support and repeats itself; the circle adds a step and returns to the start.
* **Related Terms:** [*Appeal to authority*](#50-appeal-to-authority), [*I'm entitled to my opinion*](#26-im-entitled-to-my-opinion)

### 25. Sunk cost fallacy
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From accounting: money already spent is gone, and no decision can bring it back.*

**Family:** Informal · Statistical  
**Committed when:** A strategy has failed and its defenders are counting what it cost to start.  
**The tell:** The case for continuing is entirely about the past, and the future is not mentioned.  
**Counter:** Ask what you would do if the sunk cost did not exist. That answer is the decision; the ledger is history.  

1. Continuing a failing course of action because of what has already been invested in it.
2. It works because abandoning a cost feels like admitting the cost was wasted. The loss is already real; the sentence only makes it feel avoidable.

* **Example:** *"We cannot withdraw now, after everything we have spent."* The spending is the reason the account is empty, not the reason to keep filling it.
* **Easily confused with:** *Appeal to tradition* - Tradition binds the future to the distant past; sunk cost binds it to expenses still inside the fiscal year.
* **Related Terms:** [*Post hoc ergo propter hoc*](#91-post-hoc-ergo-propter-hoc), [*Shifting baseline*](#156-shifting-baseline)

### 26. I'm entitled to my opinion
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*A legal phrase spent as an epistemic one: the right to hold a view is offered as a reason it cannot be challenged.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A factual claim has been challenged with evidence.  
**The tell:** The reply asserts a right rather than an argument, and the evidence is left standing.  
**Counter:** Grant the right and return to the claim: opinions are free, and claims are still true or false.  

1. Ending a dispute about facts by asserting the right to hold an opinion, as though the right settled the question.
2. It works because the entitlement is real. Everyone is entitled to their opinion, and nobody is entitled to their own facts - the sentence fuses the two so the second disappears into the first.

* **Example:** *"I am entitled to my opinion."* You are. The water is still poisoned, and the entitlement has not tested it.
* **Easily confused with:** *Proof by assertion* - The assertion defends the claim; the entitlement defends the person holding it.

### 29. JAQing Off
*(verb phrase / gerund)* `/ˈdʒæk.ɪŋ ɒf/` - *move*  
*Blend of "JAQ" ["Just Asking Questions"] + vulgar slang "jacking off"*

**Trigger:** A claim the speaker cannot defend has just been said plainly, and the room has noticed.  
**Tell:** Questions shaped like accusations - "just asking" - and never the claim itself. Ask for evidence and it retreats behind the question.  
**Counter:** Answer once, in writing, then ask what evidence would change their mind. The move dies when the question stops being cheaper than the answer.

*Latin: argumentum ad hominem, "argument to the person" - the target moves from the claim to the one who made it.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A claim has just been made that cannot be answered on the merits.  
**The tell:** The reply contains no content about the claim, only about the claimant.  
**Counter:** Restate the claim and ask what is wrong with it. Character is a separate question, and it can be answered later, by evidence.  

1. Answering an argument by attacking the person who advanced it rather than the claim they advanced.
2. It works because people are credulous about character: once you dislike the speaker, their sentence loses weight even when it is true. The claim is never touched, so it is never answered.

* **Example:** *"You want to talk about the audit? You were late on your taxes in 2011."* The audit is still late, and now nobody is looking at it.
* **Easily confused with:** *Tone policing* - Ad hominem attacks the person's record or character; tone policing attacks the manner of the delivery.
* **Related Terms:** [*Straw man*](#49-straw-man), [*Association fallacy*](#129-association-fallacy), [*Bulverism*](#62-bulverism)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Ad hominem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_hominem)

### 49. Straw man
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From the training dummy: a figure stuffed with straw is easier to knock down than the man holding the argument.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** An opponent's position is paraphrased before it is answered.  
**The tell:** The refutation lands cleanly, and the person refuted never said the thing.  
**Counter:** Quote the position back in its own words and ask whether the reply still lands. If it does not, the reply was aimed elsewhere.  

1. Replacing someone's position with a weaker, sillier version of it and then defeating the version you built.
2. It works because the audience rarely holds the original wording in mind. Whoever supplies the paraphrase controls what gets refuted, so an argument can be lost without ever being made.

* **Example:** *"They want to fund the libraries because they think books are free and nobody should work."* Nobody said that. The library budget passed anyway, because the argument on the table was the straw one.
* **Easily confused with:** *Motte-and-bailey fallacy* - The straw man misstates a position you reject; the motte retreats to a modest claim you never disputed.
* **Related Terms:** [*Loaded question*](#52-loaded-question), [*False dilemma*](#51-false-dilemma), [*The fallacy fallacy*](#66-the-fallacy-fallacy), [*False attribution*](#152-false-attribution)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Straw man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straw_man)

### 50. Appeal to authority
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: argumentum ad verecundiam, an appeal to deference - the argument from the shame of disagreeing with a great name.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A name with standing is available and the evidence is not.  
**The tell:** The credentials arrive before the findings, and the findings never arrive.  
**Counter:** Ask what the expert's evidence is, and whether their expertise covers this question. A title is a pointer to evidence, not a substitute for it.  

1. Treating a claim as settled because someone respected said it, in place of the evidence that made them worth respecting.
2. It works because expertise is a genuine shortcut: nobody can verify everything, so we trust the people who did. The fallacy is trusting the title outside the field, or after the field has moved.

* **Example:** *"A Nobel laureate says the water is safe."* He holds the prize in economics. The water has never met him.
* **Easily confused with:** *Circular reasoning* - Authority outsources the premises to a person; the circle outsources them to the conclusion itself.
* **Related Terms:** [*Argument from ignorance*](#54-argument-from-ignorance), [*Argumentum ad populum*](#150-argumentum-ad-populum)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Appeal to authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_authority)

### 51. False dilemma
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Also the either-or fallacy: the Greek dilemma names two horns, and the trick is to admit only two.*

**Family:** Informal · Presumption  
**Committed when:** A decision is framed as a choice between the speaker's option and a disaster.  
**The tell:** The sentence has exactly two branches, and one of them is unthinkable.  
**Counter:** Name the third option out loud. A dilemma dies the moment a listener can see a door in the wall.  

1. Presenting a choice between two options when more exist, so that refusing one is treated as choosing the other.
2. It works by shrinking the field. Most real disputes have a third, boring option - wait, fund it differently, do half of it - and the boring option is exactly what the dilemma is built to hide.

* **Example:** *"You are either with us or with the terrorists."* There is a third position - a citizen with a question about the evidence - and the sentence has just made it unsayable.
* **Easily confused with:** *False equivalence* - The dilemma collapses the field to two; equivalence claims those two weigh the same.
* **Related Terms:** [*Argument to moderation*](#93-argument-to-moderation), [*Nirvana fallacy*](#63-nirvana-fallacy)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · False dilemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_dilemma)

### 52. Loaded question
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Also the fallacy of many questions: a question that carries its conclusion in the presupposition.*

**Family:** Informal · Improper premise  
**Committed when:** The speaker wants a concession that cannot be asked for directly.  
**The tell:** The question has an assumption in it, and every answer is an admission.  
**Counter:** Answer the presupposition, not the question: "there is no plan yet, so the question does not apply."  

1. Asking a question that presupposes the very thing under dispute, so that any direct answer concedes it.
2. It works because a question feels like an invitation, not an assertion. Answering accepts its frame; refusing looks evasive. The frame is the argument.

* **Example:** *"Why does your plan keep raising costs?"* There is no plan yet. Answer the question and you have agreed there is one, and that it costs more.
* **Easily confused with:** *Circular reasoning* - Both smuggle the conclusion in; the loaded question does it through what it asks.
* **Related Terms:** [*Straw man*](#49-straw-man), [*Proof by assertion*](#24-proof-by-assertion)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Loaded question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loaded_question)

### 53. Circular reasoning
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: circulus in probando, "a circle in the proving" - the premise and the conclusion take turns holding each other up.*

**Family:** Informal · Improper premise  
**Committed when:** A conclusion is wanted and the evidence is thin.  
**The tell:** Follow the chain of because, and it arrives back where it started.  
**Counter:** Write the argument as a numbered list of premises. The moment a premise repeats the conclusion, the circle is visible.  

1. Using a claim as its own support, often with an extra step so the circle is harder to see.
2. It works because a short loop reads as an argument. Each sentence sounds like it is adding something, and the last one sounds like a conclusion because it is the last one.

* **Example:** *"The policy is sound because it is what serious people support, and serious people support it because it is sound."* Two sentences, one step each, no ground.
* **Easily confused with:** *Appeal to authority* - The circle restates itself; the appeal to authority borrows a person to stand where the premise should be.
* **Related Terms:** [*Loaded question*](#52-loaded-question), [*Proof by assertion*](#24-proof-by-assertion)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Circular reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_reasoning)

### 54. Argument from ignorance
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: argumentum ad ignorantiam - the absence of proof is offered as proof.*

**Family:** Informal · Relevance  
**Committed when:** Nobody has produced evidence either way.  
**The tell:** The conclusion rests on what has not been found.  
**Counter:** Ask who holds the burden. A claim still needs support; an absence of evidence is evidence only when the evidence would be expected.  

1. Treating a claim as true because it has not been proven false, or false because it has not been proven true.
2. It works because the burden of proof feels like a formality. Most audiences assume a claim is either established or refuted, and the sentence supplies the third option, absence, as if it settled the matter.

* **Example:** *"Nobody has proven the machines are safe."* Nobody has proven them unsafe either; the sentence treats the missing study as a finding.
* **Easily confused with:** *Appeal to authority* - Both outsource the support; ignorance to a gap, authority to a person.
* **Related Terms:** [*Loaded question*](#52-loaded-question), [*Kafka trap*](#61-kafka-trap), [*Argument from silence*](#95-argument-from-silence)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Argument from ignorance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_from_ignorance)

### 55. Tone policing
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From policing: the offence is not what was said but the manner of saying it.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A claim has landed and its content is inconvenient.  
**The tell:** The reply is about volume, spelling, or mood rather than the sentence that was said.  
**Counter:** Separate the manner from the claim and answer the claim. If the manner really was the problem, it can be raised after.  

1. Deflecting an argument by criticising how it was delivered - too angry, too shrill, too soon.
2. It works because tone is a real problem sometimes and a distraction every time. The complaint is unfalsifiable, so answering it costs a turn and concedes the frame.

* **Example:** *"I agree with the concern, but the way you said it lost the room."* The room was lost by the sentence, not the volume, and the sentence is still standing.
* **Easily confused with:** *Ad hominem* - Tone policing attacks how the speaker spoke; ad hominem attacks who the speaker is.
* **Related Terms:** [*Straw man*](#49-straw-man), [*Whataboutism*](#148-whataboutism)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Tone policing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tone_policing)

### 56. Motte-and-bailey fallacy
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From the medieval castle: a modest motte that is easy to defend, and a rich bailey that is not.*

**Family:** Informal · Presumption  
**Committed when:** The strong version of a claim has just been contested.  
**The tell:** The position shrinks under pressure and grows again in the next conversation.  
**Counter:** Name both claims and ask which one is being defended. Accepting the motte does not concede the bailey.  

1. Advancing a strong, controversial claim, then retreating to a modest, uncontroversial one when challenged - and re-advancing the strong claim once the pressure is off.
2. It works because the two claims are related. The retreat is sincere-sounding because the modest claim is genuinely held; the strong claim is never withdrawn, only set down.

* **Example:** *"Defund the police"* argued, then "we only mean reallocating the overtime budget" defended, then argued again by Thursday.
* **Easily confused with:** *Equivocation* - The motte switches between two claims; equivocation switches between two meanings of one word.
* **Related Terms:** [*Straw man*](#49-straw-man), [*Moving the goalposts*](#127-moving-the-goalposts)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Motte-and-bailey fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motte-and-bailey_fallacy)

### 57. Texas sharpshooter fallacy
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From the marksman who fires into a barn and then paints the target around the tightest cluster.*

**Family:** Informal · Faulty generalization  
**Committed when:** A large dataset is available and a story is wanted.  
**The tell:** The hypothesis was formed after the data was seen, and this is not mentioned.  
**Counter:** Ask what the prediction was before the data arrived, and what the same measure shows in the cases not chosen.  

1. Finding a pattern in data after the fact, then presenting the pattern as though it had been predicted.
2. It works because a cluster really is a cluster. With enough data, some grouping will be tight; the fallacy is the order of operations, which nobody can see in the finished result.

* **Example:** *"Crimes cluster around these three blocks."* So does every address in the city, when you draw the box around three blocks.
* **Easily confused with:** *Cherry picking* - The sharpshooter keeps all the data and moves the target; the cherry picker keeps the target and drops the data.
* **Related Terms:** [*Post hoc ergo propter hoc*](#91-post-hoc-ergo-propter-hoc), [*McNamara fallacy*](#67-mcnamara-fallacy)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Texas sharpshooter fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_sharpshooter_fallacy)

### 58. Poisoning the well
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From the siege tactic: foul the water before the argument arrives, and it never has to be fought.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** Someone is about to be heard whose account would be inconvenient.  
**The tell:** The discrediting arrives before the claim, and usually about something the audience cannot check.  
**Counter:** Name the move, then judge the claim on its evidence. Whatever the source did, the claim is still either true or false.  

1. Discrediting a source in advance, so that anything they say afterwards is discounted before it is heard.
2. It works because a prior doubt is hard to unhear. The audience now listens for confirmation of the warning rather than for the content, and the content is never engaged.

* **Example:** *"Before you read that report, remember who wrote it."* The report is unread and already dismissed; nobody has said what is wrong in it.
* **Easily confused with:** *Ad hominem* - Poisoning the well attacks in advance and by implication; ad hominem answers something that was actually said.
* **Related Terms:** [*Appeal to motive*](#130-appeal-to-motive), [*Tu quoque*](#147-tu-quoque)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Poisoning the well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisoning_the_well)

### 59. Nut-picking
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From "nut" in the American sense: cherry picking the most extreme case and calling it the group.*

**Family:** Informal · Faulty generalization  
**Committed when:** A group's worst member is easier to find than its actual position.  
**The tell:** The reply is a screenshot of someone unrepresentative, presented as the movement's face.  
**Counter:** Ask whether the case is typical, and who speaks for the group. If nobody does, the loudest member is a poor proxy.  

1. Judging a movement by its worst adherents, or defending it by its most reasonable ones.
2. It works because the extreme case is vivid and the movement is large. Any sizeable group contains someone who will embarrass it, so the example is always available and never representative.

* **Example:** *"Look at this comment, this is what they all think."* One comment, four hundred thousand readers, no survey.
* **Easily confused with:** *Cherry picking* - Nut-picking is cherry picking where the cherries are people, chosen for being the worst available.
* **Related Terms:** [*Hasty generalization*](#142-hasty-generalization), [*Texas sharpshooter fallacy*](#57-texas-sharpshooter-fallacy)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Nutpicking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutpicking)

### 60. Sealioning
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From the webcomic: a sea lion follows you home, politely demanding that you justify yourself, forever.*

**Family:** Informal · Relevance  
**Committed when:** Someone has made a point and stopped engaging.  
**The tell:** A polite question arrives, then another, each one requiring homework, none of them answering anything.  
**Counter:** Name the pattern, not the message: state that the questions are endless and invite one. Answering the tenth is what keeps it going.  

1. Demanding endless evidence and civility from an opponent while offering none, so the exchange becomes the punishment.
2. It works because every single request is reasonable. Only the sequence is the tactic, and a sequence is invisible in any one message - which is why it is so hard to call out without looking rude.

* **Example:** *"I am just asking for a source. And for the methodology. And for the raw data. And for your credentials."* Four polite requests, one derailed argument.
* **Easily confused with:** *Shifting the burden of proof* - The shift hands over the burden once; sealioning hands it over indefinitely, one request at a time.
* **Related Terms:** [*Loaded question*](#52-loaded-question), [*Argument from ignorance*](#54-argument-from-ignorance)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Sealioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sealioning)

### 61. Kafka trap
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Named for Kafka's The Trial: the accusation is the process, and the defence is the evidence.*

**Family:** Informal · Presumption  
**Committed when:** An accusation has been made that nobody could disprove, and the accused denies it.  
**The tell:** The denial is entered as proof. A second denial is entered as proof of the first.  
**Counter:** Ask what evidence would count as innocence, and refuse to move until somebody names one. A charge with no acquittal condition is not a charge; it is a status.  

1. The structure in which denial is treated as confirmation: you are guilty, and your objection is what a guilty person would say.
2. It works because the test is unfalsifiable by design. Any response - silence, anger, calm, evidence - can be read as consistent with guilt, so the accusation can never be closed and never has to be proved.

* **Example:** *"He denied it, which is exactly what someone with something to hide would do."*
* **Easily confused with:** *Argument from ignorance* - The argument from ignorance says the claim has not been proved; the Kafka trap says the denial proves it.
* **Related Terms:** [*Poisoning the well*](#58-poisoning-the-well), [*Thought-terminating cliché*](#151-thought-terminating-cliche)
* **Source:** [Unstrange Mind · Kafka trap](https://unstrangemind.com/kafka-trap/)

### 62. Bulverism
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Coined by C. S. Lewis in 1941: the habit of assuming a person is wrong and then explaining, at leisure, why they came to be so wrong.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** An argument has been made, and its psychology is easier to discuss than its evidence.  
**The tell:** The reply explains why you believe it before establishing that it is false. The cause of the belief is offered in place of the truth of it.  
**Counter:** Ask for the refutation first and the diagnosis second. A belief can have an unflattering cause and still be correct, so the cause is not the answer.  

1. Refuting an argument by explaining why the person holds it: their class, their income, their resentments, their reading list.
2. It works because causes are always available and usually unflattering. Any position can be given a psychology, and a psychology is much easier to produce than a counter-argument.

* **Example:** *"Of course he thinks the zoning reform is fine - he bought his house in 1998."* The reform is still on the table.
* **Easily confused with:** *Ad hominem* - Ad hominem attacks the arguer; Bulverism explains the arguer and treats the explanation as the refutation.
* **Related Terms:** [*Appeal to motive*](#130-appeal-to-motive), [*Poisoning the well*](#58-poisoning-the-well)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Bulverism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulverism)

### 63. Nirvana fallacy
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*English, from the Buddhist state of perfect peace: the comparison of a real proposal with an unreal alternative.*

**Family:** Informal · Presumption  
**Committed when:** A policy has been proposed that would improve something, and somebody has found a flaw in it.  
**The tell:** The objection is measured against perfection and the status quo is measured against nothing. "It won't fix everything" is offered as a reason to do nothing.  
**Counter:** Ask what happens without it, and compare the two. A proposal is not competing with an ideal; it is competing with the world it would replace.  

1. Rejecting a measure because it is imperfect, while treating the present arrangement as though it had no cost.
2. It works because the status quo is the invisible baseline. Nobody has to defend the current waiting list, the current price, or the current number of deaths - so any reform carries the burden of proof and the alternative carries none.

* **Example:** *"The bill only covers half the uninsured, so it doesn't really solve the problem."* Half the uninsured is the comparison, not the bill.
* **Easily confused with:** *False dilemma* - The false dilemma offers two options and hides a third; the Nirvana fallacy offers two and makes one of them perfect.
* **Related Terms:** [*Appeal to the stone*](#155-appeal-to-the-stone), [*Argument to moderation*](#93-argument-to-moderation)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Nirvana fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirvana_fallacy)

### 64. Base rate fallacy
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From the statistical base rate: how common a thing is before you know anything else about the particular case.*

**Family:** Informal · Statistical  
**Committed when:** A vivid case is on the table and the question of how common it is has not been asked.  
**The tell:** The number of incidents is quoted and the population is not. The story is true, the inference from it is not, and nobody multiplies.  
**Counter:** Ask out of how many, and what the rate was before. A rise from two to four is a doubling and it is also two people.  

1. Judging the frequency of an event by the salience of one instance, or by a test result, while ignoring how rare the event was to begin with.
2. It works because the vivid case is the one that reaches you. Selection decides what you see, so the sample in your head is not the sample in the world - and the denominator is the part of the story that never travels.

* **Example:** *"Three of them in one month."* Three out of how many, in a city of how many, against a five-year average of what?
* **Easily confused with:** *Anecdotal evidence* - Anecdotal evidence argues from one case; the base rate fallacy argues from one case while a rate sits unread beside it.
* **Related Terms:** [*Survivorship bias*](#128-survivorship-bias), [*Hasty generalization*](#142-hasty-generalization)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Base rate fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_rate_fallacy)

### 65. Appeal to force
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: argumentum ad baculum, the argument to the stick - the reason offered with the implication of what happens if it is refused.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** An argument is being lost, and the person losing it has something the other person needs.  
**The tell:** The conclusion arrives with a consequence attached: the funding, the permit, the job, the invitation, the quiet life.  
**Counter:** Name the consequence out loud and separate it from the reason. A conclusion does not become true because disagreeing with it is expensive.  

1. Persuasion by threat: the merits are not addressed because the cost of dissent has already been made clear.
2. It works because it does not need to be stated. A reminder of who signs the contract is cheaper than an argument and much harder to complain about - and the person who complains has just demonstrated that they understood it.

* **Example:** *"I'd think carefully about how this looks for the department."* The policy was not discussed again.
* **Easily confused with:** *Appeal to fear* - Appeal to fear sells a threat to the audience; the appeal to force applies a threat to the person in the room.
* **Related Terms:** [*Heckler's veto*](#45-hecklers-veto), [*Appeal to consequences*](#134-appeal-to-consequences)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Appeal to force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_force)

### 66. The fallacy fallacy
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*English, from the informal-logic literature: the mistake of thinking that a bad argument makes a false conclusion.*

**Family:** Informal · Presumption  
**Committed when:** An argument has been shown to contain a named fallacy, and the conclusion is being discarded on that basis.  
**The tell:** The fallacy is named with confidence and the conclusion is never examined. "That's a slippery slope" ends the discussion about the slope.  
**Counter:** Separate the argument from the claim, then test the claim on its own. A conclusion can be true and badly defended, and a named fallacy is not a refutation.  

1. Concluding that a claim is false because the argument offered for it is invalid.
2. It works because naming a fallacy is cheap and sounds decisive. The name does the work of an argument while leaving the actual question exactly where it was - which is why it is popular with people who want to win without reading.

* **Example:** *"That's an appeal to authority, so the study doesn't count."* The study is still a study.
* **Easily confused with:** *Straw man* - The straw man misrepresents the argument; the fallacy fallacy accepts the argument as given and then discards the conclusion with it.
* **Related Terms:** [*Ad hominem*](#48-ad-hominem), [*Thought-terminating cliché*](#151-thought-terminating-cliche)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Fallacy fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallacy_fallacy)

### 67. McNamara fallacy
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Named for Robert McNamara's defence metrics: body counts offered as the measure of a war the counts could not describe.*

**Family:** Informal · Statistical  
**Committed when:** A thing has to be judged, and only part of it can be counted.  
**The tell:** The measurable part is reported to two decimal places and the unmeasured part is called anecdote. The dashboard is complete and the picture is missing.  
**Counter:** Ask what the metric leaves out, and who decided it was the metric. A number is a decision about what counts, made before the counting starts.  

1. Treating what can be measured as what matters: the waiting time is measured, the diagnosis is not; the arrest is counted, the charge is not.
2. It works because numbers are auditable and judgements are not. A figure can be defended line by line, so the institution reports the figure - and the thing that could not be counted slowly stops being the thing that is managed.

* **Example:** *"Response times are down 12%."* The response is faster and the ambulance no longer comes.
* **Easily confused with:** *Texas sharpshooter fallacy* - The sharpshooter picks the target after the shot; the McNamara fallacy picks the target because it is the one that can be scored.
* **Related Terms:** [*Survivorship bias*](#128-survivorship-bias), [*Appeal to authority*](#50-appeal-to-authority)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · McNamara fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McNamara_fallacy)

### 68. Hypocontortionist
*(noun)* `/ˌhɪp.oʊ.kənˈtɔːr.ʃən.ɪst/` - *species*  
*Blend of "hypocrite" + "contortionist"*

**Occasion:** A principle the speaker has just been caught applying to somebody else.  
**Call:** "That was different."  
**Tell:** Principles that bend at the wallet · Public austerity, private indulgence · Repents only after the photo  
**Counter:** Ask what the difference is, and who it protects. The contortion only shows when the same rule is read twice.  

1. The pattern of holding a principle against opponents and releasing it for allies, with the release performed in public and never mentioned again.
2. It works because most principles are asserted rather than written down, so the exemption never has to be argued - only survived. The tell is not the reversal but the speed.

* **Example:** *"After spending years screaming about 'law and order' and 'treason,' the **hypocontortionist** instantly folded himself into a pretzel to insist that Trump's latest authoritarian threat was 'obviously just a joke.'"*
* **Easily confused with:** *Martyrholic* - The hypocontortionist bends the principle; the martyrholic weeps about being judged for it.

### 69. Mythstorian
*(noun)* `/ˈmɪθˌstɔːr.i.ən/` - *species*  
*Blend of "myth" + "historian"*

**Occasion:** A policy argument that has just been moved from the present into a golden age.  
**Call:** "In my day -"  
**Tell:** Nostalgic for a decade it cannot date · Sanitized, never sourced · Confuses a monument with a record  
**Counter:** Ask for the year, the statute, and the source. Nostalgia cannot survive a date.  

1. The pattern of moving an argument out of the present and into a golden age: the policy defended by a past that did not happen.
2. It works because nostalgia is unfalsifiable by default - nobody was there - until somebody produces the year, the statute, and the source.

* **Example:** *"Trying to ban primary historical documents while claiming the past was a peaceful utopia without systemic injustice is pure **mythstorian** revisionism."*

### 70. Bootcurious
*(noun / adjective)* `/ˈbuːtˌkjʊər.i.əs/` - *buff*  
*Blend of "bootlicker" + "curious"*

**Effect:** Excuses any contradiction for the length of one news cycle, and relabels deference as independence.  
**Tell:** "I'm socially liberal, but -" arrives before the argument does. Reads exactly one newsletter, and finds the boot reasonable.

### 89. Unelected official

### 90. Two wrongs make a right
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From the proverb, inverted: the move takes a well-known warning and uses it as a defence.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** One's own side has just been caught doing something.  
**The tell:** The answer is a comparison, and the comparison is accurate.  
**Counter:** Point out that both acts are still wrong, and that the ledger being even is not the same as it being clean.  

1. Defending an act by pointing at an equivalent act on the other side, as though symmetry settled the question.
2. It works because fairness is a stronger instinct than consistency. The hearer wants the ledger balanced, and the sentence offers balance without ever asking whether either entry was wrong.

* **Example:** *"They gerrymandered too."* Both maps are still rigged; the sentence has only arranged for nobody to fix either.
* **Easily confused with:** *Ad hominem* - The tu quoque changes the subject to the accuser's conduct; ad hominem changes it to the accuser's character.
* **Related Terms:** [*Special pleading*](#92-special-pleading), [*Whataboutism*](#148-whataboutism)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Two wrongs make a right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_wrongs_make_a_right)

### 91. Post hoc ergo propter hoc
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: "after this, therefore because of this" - the first reason children believe the rooster makes the sun rise.*

**Family:** Informal · Questionable cause  
**Committed when:** Two events happened in sequence and a cause is being claimed.  
**The tell:** The evidence is the calendar, not the mechanism.  
**Counter:** Ask for the mechanism and the comparison: what happened where the first event did not occur?  

1. Concluding that one event caused another because it happened first.
2. It works because sequence is the one causal cue an audience can observe directly. Order is visible; mechanism has to be argued, so order wins by default.

* **Example:** *"I took the supplement and the cough cleared in a week."* A week is also how long a cough lasts.
* **Easily confused with:** *Cherry picking* - Post hoc invents a cause from sequence; cherry picking hides the cases where the sequence did not hold.
* **Related Terms:** [*Slippery slope*](#20-slippery-slope), [*Hasty generalization*](#142-hasty-generalization)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Post hoc ergo propter hoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post_hoc_ergo_propter_hoc)

### 92. Special pleading
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From law: a plea that asks the court to apply a different rule to this case, with no reason given for the exception.*

**Family:** Informal · Presumption  
**Committed when:** A rule the speaker has just applied to someone else would now apply to the speaker.  
**The tell:** The rule holds, except here, for reasons that are never stated as a rule.  
**Counter:** Ask what makes this case different in a way that could be written down and applied to everyone.  

1. Applying a standard to others while exempting oneself, without giving a reason that would not also exempt the others.
2. It works because the exemption is usually framed as context. Every rule has context; the fallacy is invoking it only for one side.

* **Example:** *"Attacking a public official's family is out of bounds."* Said by the account that spent last spring on a mayor's brother.
* **Easily confused with:** *Two wrongs make a right* - Special pleading asks for an exemption from the standard; two wrongs argues the standard is void because someone else broke it.
* **Related Terms:** [*Moving the goalposts*](#127-moving-the-goalposts), [*No true Scotsman*](#21-no-true-scotsman)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Special pleading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_pleading)

### 93. Argument to moderation
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Also the middle ground fallacy: the midpoint between two positions is treated as the truth by construction.*

**Family:** Informal · Presumption  
**Committed when:** Two positions are on the table and the speaker wants to look reasonable.  
**The tell:** The recommendation is arithmetic - halfway - rather than an account of what is true.  
**Counter:** Ask which position the evidence supports. A compromise between a measured claim and a fabricated one is a partial fabrication.  

1. Assuming that the correct answer must lie between two stated positions, regardless of what either position says.
2. It works because compromise is a genuine virtue in negotiation. The sentence borrows that virtue for questions of fact, where the midpoint of true and false is still false.

* **Example:** *"One side says the water is safe, the other says it is poison - surely the truth is in the middle."* The test results are not in the middle; they are in the lab.
* **Easily confused with:** *False equivalence* - Equivalence says the two sides are equally wrong; moderation says the truth is arithmetically between them.
* **Related Terms:** [*False dilemma*](#51-false-dilemma), [*Nirvana fallacy*](#63-nirvana-fallacy)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Argument to moderation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_to_moderation)

### 94. False balance
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Also bothsidesism: the studio graph with two guests, as though the shape of the panel were the shape of the evidence.*

**Family:** Informal · Statistical  
**Committed when:** One position is supported by the evidence and the other by its existence.  
**The tell:** The two sides get the same airtime, and the audience infers the same standing.  
**Counter:** Ask what the evidence weighs, not what the panel looks like. Balance is about who gets heard; accuracy is about what is true.  

1. Presenting two sides as equally supported when the evidence is lopsided, because a balanced panel feels fairer than an accurate one.
2. It works because fairness is a real journalistic virtue and symmetry looks like it. But a coin that lands heads ninety times out of a hundred is not evenly disputed, and equal time misrepresents it as such.

* **Example:** *"Some say the bridge is safe, others say it will fall."* One of those groups has the inspection report.
* **Easily confused with:** *False equivalence* - Equivalence says the two claims are alike; false balance gives them alike treatment and lets the audience draw the first conclusion.
* **Related Terms:** [*Argument to moderation*](#93-argument-to-moderation), [*Bothsidesing*](#37-bothsidesing)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · False balance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_balance)

### 95. Argument from silence
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: argumentum ex silentio - nothing was said, so the nothing is read as a statement.*

**Family:** Informal · Relevance  
**Committed when:** A record is missing and a conclusion is convenient.  
**The tell:** The argument rests on what nobody wrote down.  
**Counter:** Ask whether anyone would have written it down. If the answer is no, the silence is not evidence of anything.  

1. Concluding something from the absence of a record, when the record would not have mentioned it either way.
2. It works because silence is genuinely informative sometimes. Whether it is depends entirely on whether the silence was supposed to be broken, and that question is the one the sentence skips.

* **Example:** *"No complaints were filed, so everyone was satisfied."* Nobody knew there was a form.
* **Easily confused with:** *Argument from ignorance* - Ignorance is about missing evidence in general; silence is about a specific record that was never kept.
* **Related Terms:** [*Survivorship bias*](#128-survivorship-bias), [*Shifting the burden of proof*](#153-shifting-the-burden-of-proof)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Argument from silence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_from_silence)

### 126. Appeal to fear
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: argumentum ad metum. The oldest political argument, and the one with the shortest feedback loop.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** Something frightening is adjacent to the decision, and the case for the proposal is weak.  
**The tell:** The argument ends in a threat, and the threat is doing all the work.  
**Counter:** Ask for the proposed mechanism, then for the probability. Fear is a reason to look; it is not a reason to conclude.  

1. Arguing for a course of action by amplifying what will happen if it is refused, rather than by showing that it will work.
2. It works because fear narrows attention. A frightened audience stops asking whether the proposal is good and starts asking whether the alternative is survivable.

* **Example:** *"If we do nothing, they win."* Nothing is not on the ballot; the question was the budget line, which is a different sentence about a different thing.
* **Easily confused with:** *Appeal to emotion* - Fear is a channel of the wider emotional appeal, and the one that most reliably skips the argument.
* **Related Terms:** [*Slippery slope*](#20-slippery-slope), [*Appeal to force*](#65-appeal-to-force)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Appeal to fear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_fear)

### 127. Moving the goalposts
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From the pitch: the shot went in, so the posts are moved back and the point does not count.*

**Family:** Informal · Presumption  
**Committed when:** The evidence asked for has just been supplied.  
**The tell:** The standard changes at the moment it is satisfied, and only then.  
**Counter:** Fix the standard in writing before the evidence is gathered. If the standard moves again, the argument is over, and say so.  

1. Demanding new evidence after the original demand has been met.
2. It works because each new request sounds reasonable on its own. Nobody agrees to an impossible standard all at once; they agree to a series of fair-sounding ones.

* **Example:** *"Show me the emails."* The emails are produced. "Emails can be edited - show me the metadata." The metadata would not have been mentioned yesterday.
* **Easily confused with:** *No true Scotsman* - The Scotsman moves the group boundary; the goalposts move the evidentiary one.
* **Related Terms:** [*Special pleading*](#92-special-pleading), [*Nirvana fallacy*](#63-nirvana-fallacy)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Moving the goalposts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moving_the_goalposts)

### 128. Survivorship bias
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From the aircraft that returned: the armour study nearly went to the wings, until someone asked about the planes that did not come back.*

**Family:** Informal · Statistical  
**Committed when:** A set of successes is being used to explain why a method works.  
**The tell:** Every case you can see succeeded, and nothing explains what happened to the others.  
**Counter:** Ask where the failures went. If the answer is "they are not in the dataset", the dataset is the argument.  

1. Drawing a conclusion from the cases that are visible while ignoring the cases that are missing because they failed.
2. It works because the missing cases are missing. The sample looks complete precisely because the ones that would have completed it are not there to be counted.

* **Example:** *"Every founder I know says the degree was useless."* The founders who dropped out and failed are not at the conferences where this sentence gets said.
* **Easily confused with:** *Cherry picking* - Cherry picking keeps the data and drops the rest; survivorship bias never had the rest - it was never recorded.
* **Related Terms:** [*Anecdotal evidence*](#145-anecdotal-evidence), [*Base rate fallacy*](#64-base-rate-fallacy)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Survivorship bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survivorship_bias)

### 129. Association fallacy
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Also guilt by association: the company a claim keeps is treated as the claim itself.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** An idea's supporters are easier to attack than the idea.  
**The tell:** The response names the company and never the claim.  
**Counter:** Ask what the claim says and whether it is true. Who else believes it is a separate question with a separate answer.  

1. Rejecting or accepting an idea because of who else holds it, rather than because of what it says.
2. It works because groups really do cluster. Sharing a conclusion with a bad actor is genuine information about the actor, and none at all about whether the conclusion is true.

* **Example:** *"That is the same thing the crank down the street says."* The crank is also right about the weather, and nobody checks the sky by checking him.
* **Easily confused with:** *Ad hominem* - The association attacks the company the claim keeps; ad hominem attacks the person who made it.
* **Related Terms:** [*Hasty generalization*](#142-hasty-generalization), [*Poisoning the well*](#58-poisoning-the-well)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Association fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_fallacy)

### 130. Appeal to motive
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Also the circumstantial ad hominem: the argument is answered with an account of why the arguer wants it to be true.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A claim has been made by someone with an obvious interest in it.  
**The tell:** The reply is entirely about who benefits, and the claim's evidence is not mentioned.  
**Counter:** Separate interest from evidence: ask what the numbers say, then ask who profits. Both questions have answers and only one is about truth.  

1. Dismissing a claim by attributing a motive to whoever made it, as though the motive settled the facts.
2. It works because motives are real and often relevant. But a motive explains why someone is talking, not whether what they said is accurate - and the audience conflates the two.

* **Example:** *"Of course the landlord says rents are fine - he owns the building."* He does; the rent roll is still a document.
* **Easily confused with:** *Ad hominem* - Appeal to motive attacks the reason behind the claim; ad hominem attacks the person's character or record.
* **Related Terms:** [*Poisoning the well*](#58-poisoning-the-well), [*Bulverism*](#62-bulverism)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Appeal to motive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_motive)

### 131. Appeal to pity
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: argumentum ad misericordiam - the claim is supported by how much its speaker has suffered.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A claim or a failing needs support and the speaker has a sympathetic history.  
**The tell:** The case rests on what it cost the speaker, not on what the evidence shows.  
**Counter:** Separate the hardship from the question: acknowledge the cost, then ask what the claim actually says.  

1. Arguing that a claim is true, or an act excusable, because of the hardship of the person making it.
2. It works because sympathy is a real moral response and the hardship is often genuine. Neither fact tells you whether the claim is accurate.

* **Example:** *"I lost my job over this, so you know I am telling the truth."* Losing a job is terrible and says nothing about the facts.
* **Easily confused with:** *Appeal to emotion* - Pity is the specific channel; the emotional appeal is the whole family.
* **Related Terms:** [*Tu quoque*](#147-tu-quoque)

### 132. Ad nauseam
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: "to the point of sickness" - the argument that wins by making the audience tired.*

**Family:** Informal · Relevance  
**Committed when:** A claim has been answered once and the answer was not accepted.  
**The tell:** The same sentence returns unchanged, and the replies get shorter.  
**Counter:** Answer once, note that the answer stands, and stop. Repetition is a contest of stamina, and it can be declined.  

1. Repeating a claim until objection feels pointless, and treating the exhaustion as agreement.
2. It works on volume rather than content. Nobody can answer the same sentence forty times, so the fortieth repetition lands unanswered and looks unanswerable.

* **Example:** *"It was the biggest crowd ever. Biggest crowd. Everyone agrees it was the biggest crowd."* Three sentences, one claim, no count.
* **Easily confused with:** *Proof by assertion* - Proof by assertion repeats to sound certain; ad nauseam repeats to make the audience stop asking.
* **Related Terms:** [*Gish gallop*](#154-gish-gallop), [*Thought-terminating cliché*](#151-thought-terminating-cliche)

### 133. Appeal to novelty
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: argumentum ad novitatem - the newest thing is treated as the best thing, and the year as an argument.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A practice or claim is being defended and its only advantage is being recent.  
**The tell:** The support is a date: it is 2026, nobody does that anymore.  
**Counter:** Ask what changed. If nothing changed in the evidence, the calendar is not an argument.  

1. Treating a claim or a practice as correct because it is new, or an older one as wrong because it is old.
2. It works because progress is real and the future usually arrives. Recency is genuine evidence of relevance; it is not evidence of correctness, and the two are easy to confuse in a feed.

* **Example:** *"Nobody uses cash anymore."* Most people still do; the sentence is about the future the speaker would like.
* **Easily confused with:** *Appeal to tradition* - Tradition cites age as proof; novelty cites youth as proof. Both substitute a timestamp for a reason.
* **Related Terms:** [*Appeal to nature*](#23-appeal-to-nature), [*Argumentum ad populum*](#150-argumentum-ad-populum)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Appeal to novelty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_novelty)

### 134. Appeal to consequences
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: argumentum ad consequentiam - the belief is judged by how it would feel for it to be true.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A claim has implications the speaker cannot accept.  
**The tell:** The reply describes what would happen if the claim were true, and stops there.  
**Counter:** Separate the two questions: what is true, and what to do about it. Disliking the second answer does not change the first.  

1. Arguing that a claim is false because its consequences would be unpleasant, or true because they would be convenient.
2. It works because consequences are legitimately part of policy - just not of truth. The sentence moves from what follows to what is, and the move is one word wide.

* **Example:** *"If that were true, the whole policy would be indefensible."* It may well be indefensible; the audit found what it found.
* **Easily confused with:** *Appeal to fear* - Fear forecasts the bad outcome to end the argument; consequences use the bad outcome as a disproof.
* **Related Terms:** [*Slippery slope*](#20-slippery-slope), [*Just-world hypothesis*](#159-just-world-hypothesis)

### 135. Passoffist
*(noun)* `/ˈpæs.ɒf.ɪst/` - *species*  
*Blend of "pass off" + "pacifist" - the peacemaker whose peace is the subject never coming up*

**Occasion:** A table where the subject has just arrived, and one person has decided the evening will not be about it.  
**Call:** "Let's not make this political."  
**Tell:** Has a view on everything except the thing just said · Asks both sides to calm down, and means one · Calls the argument the problem, never the claim  
**Counter:** Ask what they think, not what should be done. Neutrality is a position on someone else's fight, and it survives only while the question stays about them: put it in the first person and the passoffist has to pick a side, which is the thing the mediating was for.  

1. The pattern of keeping a subject off the table while looking fair about it: the topic changed, the link not opened, the dinner kept pleasant, the view never tested because it was never said.
2. It works because a mediator is handed authority over a dispute without having to enter it, so the passoffist gets the standing of a neutral and the safety of a partisan - a seat at the table that never requires a vote.

* **Example:** *"Asked what she thought of the removals, the **passoffist** observed that everyone was getting very heated, and suggested they talk about something else."*
* **Easily confused with:** *Decorumancer* - Both end the argument without answering it: the decorumancer objects to the volume, the passoffist to the subject.

### 136. Provocrop
*(noun)* `/ˈprɒv.ə.krɒp/` - *species*  
*Blend of "provocation" + "sharecropper" - works a field of someone else's outrage and keeps a share of the crop*

**Occasion:** A merchandise table at the back of a rally, and a slogan that was a headline this morning.  
**Call:** "It's just a shirt."  
**Tell:** Wears the argument instead of making it · Knows the slogan, not the policy behind it · Buys the take from the people who sell it  
**Counter:** Ask what they would vote for, not what they would wear. The provocrop holds the position on loan - the crop is someone else's, worn for the charge it carries - and a question about the policy calls in the rent.  

1. The pattern of carrying a provocation as merchandise: the slogan on the chest, the flag on the truck, the insult on the bumper - worn for the charge it carries rather than for anything it commits the wearer to.
2. It works because a shirt is a claim with no obligation attached. The take arrives ready-made, the reaction is immediate, nothing is asked of the wearer, and the rent for all of it is paid in what the room now finds normal.

* **Example:** *"Tucked into dad jeans at the hardware store, the **provocrop** was wearing this morning's headline and had not read past it."*

### 137. Precedentarian
*(noun)* `/ˌpres.ɪˈden.tɛə.ri.ən/` - *species*  
*Blend of "precedent" + "authoritarian" - the one authority that never has to run for anything*

**Occasion:** A budget line, a bylaw, or a rota that nobody in the room can remember choosing.  
**Call:** "This is how we have always done it."  
**Tell:** Can name the year, never the reason · Answers what was decided when the question is what it was for · Treats a precedent as proof the question is closed  
**Counter:** Ask for the decision, not the tradition: who signed it, what it replaced, and what it cost. A precedent that survives the minutes is a rule; one that does not was a habit, and habits are not authority.  

1. The pattern of treating the last decision as the decision: the practice nobody chose, defended because it exists, with the question closed by the fact that somebody answered it once.
2. It works because continuity is worth something and memory is short. Most rules do carry a reason, so the precedentarian is right often enough never to have to say which ones - the burden lands on you to prove this is the exception, and the minutes are in a basement.

* **Example:** *"Asked why the roster worked that way, the **precedentarian** said it had been like that since 1994, which was true, and which nobody had checked."*
* **Easily confused with:** *Biasographer* - The biasographer writes the flattering account; the precedentarian cites it as a constraint.

### 138. Soft launch
*(noun / verb)* `/sɒft lɔːntʃ/` - *move*  
*English: from software, where a product ships quietly to see whether it breaks before anyone is told it exists.*

**Trigger:** A change that would be contested if it were announced and unremarkable if it were not.  
**Tell:** A form that quietly gains a field, a fee that appears on the next statement, a policy page edited on a Friday with no changelog.  
**Counter:** Ask for the changelog. Every quiet change has an author, a date and a diff; publish the diff on a schedule and the soft launch becomes an announcement with worse timing.  

1. The move of shipping a contested change without announcing it: no speech, no press release, a line on a form and a date already in the past, and by the time anyone notices they are living under the arrangement.
2. It works because implementation beats argument. An announcement invites a hearing, a comment period and a vote; a deployment invites a complaint from whoever happens to meet it, and the people who would have organised against it find out one at a time.

* **Example:** *"The **soft launch** was a checkbox on page four of a renewal form; by the time the council asked who approved it, four thousand people had already agreed."*

### 139. Grandfather clause
*(noun)* `/ˈɡræn.fɑː.ðə klɔːz/` - *ability*  
*American: from the literacy tests that exempted any man whose grandfather had voted - the exemption was the point and the test was the alibi.*

**Effect:** Keeps the existing practice legal while the new rule binds everyone who arrives after it, so the baseline never actually moves.  
**Available to:** Any legislature that wants a reform to look larger than the number of people it covers.  
**Counter:** Date the exemption. A grandfather clause with an end date is a transition; one without is a permanent carve-out in a reform's clothes, and the difference is a line nobody reads.  

1. The legislative move that exempts what is already there: the rule applies to the future, the present keeps its permission, and the practice the rule was written about survives because it arrived first.
2. It works because transition relief is reasonable. Every real reform lets people finish what they started, and the grandfather clause arrives wearing that reason - when the transition ends is the only thing that separates the two, and that date is never in the text.

* **Example:** *"The **grandfather clause** exempted every plant already built, which was every plant, so the rule applied to a future that had not been scheduled."*
* **Easily confused with:** *The carve-out* - The carve-out writes the exception into the rule; the grandfather clause writes the rule around what already exists.

### 140. Industry conference
*(noun)* `/ˈɪn.də.stri ˈkɒn.fər.əns/` - *habitat*  
*English: a conference is where an industry agrees with itself in public, and the programme is the agreement.*

**Carriers:** The breakout session, the sponsored coffee break, the panel titled "Navigating the New Normal", the lanyard that says who paid for lunch.  
**Economics:** Exhibit fees, sponsored sessions, and a regulator on the panel who also sits on the advisory board.  
**Signature:** A practice that was a scandal in last year's programme is a best practice in this one, and two of the three speakers are the same.  
**Counter:** Read the programme from three years ago. The room does not change its mind; it changes its slide deck, and the archive is the only record of what it used to say.  

1. The room where a practice becomes respectable: a programme, a sponsor, a panel, and the phrase "industry standard" doing the work an argument would have had to do.

* **Example:** *"Last year the **industry conference** ran a session on avoiding the practice; this year it ran a keynote on scaling it, with two of the same three speakers."*

### 141. Outrage fatigue
*(noun)* `/ˈaʊt.reɪdʒ fəˈtiːɡ/` - *buff*  
*English: from alarm fatigue, the ward's habit of turning down a monitor that cries wolf - the fourth alarm gets a slower answer than the first.*

**Effect:** Makes each fresh revelation land softer than the last, so the same conduct can be disclosed three times and count once.  
**Tell:** The second story about the same thing gets a shorter article, and the third gets a line in the round-up.  
**Expires:** Never on its own: it ends when something new arrives to be outraged about, which is the day the old conduct becomes the background.  

1. A condition in which the audience has spent its capacity to be shocked: the disclosure that would have ended a career in March is a paragraph in September, and the conduct has not changed.
2. It works because attention is finite and disclosures are not. Nobody decides to stop caring; the caring is allocated to the newest thing, and the pattern that produced the story keeps producing it until the stories stop being news.

* **Example:** *"By the third disclosure the **outrage fatigue** showed in the coverage: a front page, then a column, then a line in the round-up."*

### 142. Hasty generalization
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: secundum quid, "according to a particular" - a rule drawn from too narrow a sample.*

**Family:** Informal · Faulty generalization  
**Committed when:** A striking case is available and a general claim is wanted.  
**The tell:** The conclusion is far wider than the number of examples behind it.  
**Counter:** Ask how many cases, chosen how, over what period. The answer is usually one, chosen by being memorable.  

1. Drawing a broad conclusion from a sample too small, or too selected, to support it.
2. It works because a single vivid case feels like a pattern. The mind generalises from what it can picture, and one picture is enough to feel like evidence.

* **Example:** *"I met two rude tourists from there, so that is what they are like."* Two people, one airport, an entire country.
* **Easily confused with:** *Anecdotal evidence* - The generalization is the conclusion; the anecdote is the single story it was built on.

### 143. Cherry picking
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From the orchard: you pick the fruit that looks best and leave the rest on the tree for someone else to find.*

**Family:** Informal · Faulty generalization  
**Committed when:** There is a large body of evidence and a conclusion to support.  
**The tell:** Each cited fact checks out; the pattern only holds because of what is missing.  
**Counter:** Ask for the denominator: the same measure over the same period, including the years that argue the other way.  

1. Selecting the evidence that supports a conclusion while ignoring the evidence that does not.
2. It works because every selection is individually true. Nothing in the sentence is a lie, so the audit has to ask what was left out - and the audience rarely sees the tree.

* **Example:** *"Crime fell in the years we cut the programme."* It also fell in the four years before, in the neighbouring county, and by more.
* **Easily confused with:** *Texas sharpshooter fallacy* - Cherry picking removes the inconvenient data; the sharpshooter keeps all of it and moves the target instead.

### 144. False equivalence
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: false equivalence, or the apples-and-oranges move. The failure is in the comparison, not in either thing compared.*

**Family:** Informal · Presumption  
**Committed when:** Two acts are being compared and the comparison flatters the speaker's side.  
**The tell:** The two things share a category and nothing else: scale, intent, effect, and remedy are all different.  
**Counter:** Compare like quantities: how many, how bad, how permanent, who can fix it. Equivalence collapses under one honest axis.  

1. Treating two things as equally bad, or equally serious, because they share a name or a shape.
2. It works because a shared label feels like a shared scale. Once both acts are called "unfair", the sentence can weigh them evenly without ever measuring them.

* **Example:** *"Both sides traffic in misinformation."* One retracted a chart; the other broadcast a fabricated one to nine million people for a week.
* **Easily confused with:** *False dilemma* - The dilemma collapses the field to two options; equivalence collapses the distance between them.

### 145. Anecdotal evidence
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*From the Greek anekdota, "unpublished things" - the story that never made it into the record.*

**Family:** Informal · Faulty generalization  
**Committed when:** A statistic has just been presented that the speaker dislikes.  
**The tell:** The reply begins "well, in my experience" and ends the discussion.  
**Counter:** Ask whether the story is typical. One case is a reason to check the data, not a substitute for it.  

1. Using a single personal story as though it outweighed systematic evidence about the same question.
2. It works because stories are persuasive and statistics are not. One concrete case is easier to hold in mind than a distribution, so it feels heavier even when it is one data point.

* **Example:** *"My uncle smoked for sixty years."* The actuarial table is not about his uncle; it is about the twenty thousand uncles behind it.
* **Easily confused with:** *Hasty generalization* - The anecdote is the evidence; the generalization is the rule drawn from it.

### 146. Equivocation
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: aequus vox, "equal voice" - one word doing two jobs in the same argument.*

**Family:** Informal · Ambiguity  
**Committed when:** A word with more than one sense is load-bearing in the argument.  
**The tell:** The term means one thing in the premise and another in the conclusion.  
**Counter:** Define the term at first use and substitute the definition everywhere. If the argument breaks, the word was doing the work.  

1. Using one word in two different senses inside a single argument, so the conclusion appears to follow.
2. It works because the word really is the same word. The shift happens in the meaning, which is invisible on the page and audible only if you ask what the word meant each time.

* **Example:** *"Speech is free, so my platform must host it."* Free as in protected from the state, free as in provided by a company - one word, two sentences, no bridge.
* **Easily confused with:** *Motte-and-bailey fallacy* - The motte switches between two claims; equivocation switches between two senses of a word inside one claim.

### 147. Tu quoque
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: "you too" - the second-person form of the two-wrongs move, and the most common sentence on the internet.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A criticism has been made that one cannot answer on the merits.  
**The tell:** The reply is a counter-accusation, and the original charge is never mentioned again.  
**Counter:** Concede the point about consistency and keep the charge on the table - both can be true, and only one was asked about.  

1. Answering a charge by charging the accuser with the same thing, as though a second offence cancelled the first.
2. It works because hypocrisy is genuinely relevant to credibility, so the deflection has a real foothold. The foothold is about the speaker; the question was about the act.

* **Example:** *"You are funded by a billionaire."* "So are you." Both disclosures are true; only one of them was on the table.
* **Easily confused with:** *Two wrongs make a right* - Tu quoque attacks the accuser's consistency; two wrongs uses the comparison as a defence of the act itself.

### 148. Whataboutism
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Named for the Soviet reply to every complaint: "and what about…?"*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A specific question has been asked that the speaker would rather not answer.  
**The tell:** The reply begins with "what about" and never returns to the question.  
**Counter:** Say that the other case can be discussed after this one, and repeat the question. The order of questions is a fair thing to insist on.  

1. Deflecting a question by raising a different grievance, so the original subject never gets answered.
2. It works because there is always another grievance. The move does not have to be false - it only has to be more interesting than the question being asked.

* **Example:** *"What about the crime in your city?"* The question was about a contract. Both can be answered; only one was asked.
* **Easily confused with:** *Tu quoque* - Tu quoque answers a charge with a charge against the accuser; whataboutism raises any third subject at all.
* **Related Terms:** [*Ad hominem*](#48-ad-hominem), [*Two wrongs make a right*](#90-two-wrongs-make-a-right)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Whataboutism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whataboutism)

### 149. Appeal to ridicule
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Also argumentum ad ridiculum: the claim is answered with a joke about it, and the joke counts as the answer.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A claim is awkward to answer and easy to caricature.  
**The tell:** The reply gets a laugh, and the claim is not restated anywhere in it.  
**Counter:** Restate the claim in its strongest form and ask what is wrong with it. If it really is absurd, the absurdity will survive being stated fairly.  

1. Dismissing a position by making it sound absurd rather than by showing that it is wrong.
2. It works because ridicule is cheap and memorable. A laugh is a conclusion the audience reaches themselves, which makes it feel like their own judgement rather than the speaker's.

* **Example:** *"So we should just let everyone do whatever they want, then?"* Nobody proposed that; the sentence made the proposal funny and then defeated the joke.
* **Easily confused with:** *Appeal to the stone* - Ridicule gets a laugh; the stone gets a shrug. Both substitute a reaction for a reason.
* **Related Terms:** [*Straw man*](#49-straw-man), [*Tone policing*](#55-tone-policing)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Appeal to ridicule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_ridicule)

### 150. Argumentum ad populum
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: "to the people" - the crowd is treated as the evidence, and the bandwagon as the proof.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A claim is popular or unpopular and that is the strongest thing that can be said for it.  
**The tell:** The support is a headcount: everyone knows, everybody is saying, no one believes that anymore.  
**Counter:** Ask what the crowd is responding to. Majorities have been wrong about medicine, geography, and who should vote.  

1. Treating a belief as true because many people hold it, or as false because few do.
2. It works because consensus is usually a decent heuristic and occasionally the only evidence available. The fallacy is treating a majority as a measurement.

* **Example:** *"Everyone knows the water is fine."* Everyone knew a lot of things that turned out to be false; the water has a test result.
* **Easily confused with:** *Appeal to authority* - Authority borrows one credential; ad populum borrows a crowd, which is cheaper and worse.
* **Related Terms:** [*Appeal to tradition*](#22-appeal-to-tradition), [*Appeal to nature*](#23-appeal-to-nature)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Argumentum ad populum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argumentum_ad_populum)

### 151. Thought-terminating cliché
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Named by Robert Jay Lifton in his study of thought reform: a phrase that ends the thinking rather than the question.*

**Family:** Informal · Faulty generalization  
**Committed when:** A question has been raised that would take real work to answer.  
**The tell:** The reply is a proverb: everything happens for a reason, it is what it is, both sides have a point.  
**Counter:** Ask what the phrase actually asserts. Most of these sentences, unpacked, turn out to be either obvious or false.  

1. Closing a line of thought with a stock phrase that sounds like a conclusion and forecloses the next question.
2. It works because the phrase is familiar and warm. Familiarity reads as wisdom, and the sentence arrives pre-approved, so using it feels like agreeing with everyone rather than ending an argument.

* **Example:** *"It is what it is."* Said about a policy that could be changed, by the people who could change it.
* **Easily confused with:** *Proof by assertion* - The cliché stops the conversation with familiarity; the assertion stops it with confidence.
* **Related Terms:** [*Appeal to tradition*](#22-appeal-to-tradition), [*Gish gallop*](#154-gish-gallop)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Thought-terminating cliché](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought-terminating_clich%C3%A9)

### 152. False attribution
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Also quoting out of context: the words are real, the sentence they were in is gone.*

**Family:** Informal · Red herring  
**Committed when:** A quote would settle the argument and the full text would not.  
**The tell:** The quotation marks are doing the work, and the surrounding sentence has been removed.  
**Counter:** Open the source and read the paragraph. Attribution is checkable, which makes this the most falsifiable fallacy in the guide.  

1. Ascribing to someone a claim they did not make - by misquoting, by quoting a fragment, or by naming a source that never said it.
2. It works because the citation is true as far as it goes. Verifying a quote means opening the transcript, and most readers will only verify the part that already agrees with them.

* **Example:** *"They said the plan was dead on arrival."* The transcript says it was dead on arrival in the Senate, which then passed it.
* **Easily confused with:** *Straw man* - False attribution puts new words in someone's mouth; the straw man puts a weaker argument there.
* **Related Terms:** [*Cherry picking*](#143-cherry-picking), [*Association fallacy*](#129-association-fallacy)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · False attribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_attribution)

### 153. Shifting the burden of proof
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: onus probandi. The burden sits with whoever makes the claim - and the shift is the act of handing it over.*

**Family:** Informal · Presumption  
**Committed when:** A claim has been made with no evidence offered.  
**The tell:** The reply is "prove me wrong" or "do your own research", and the original claim is never defended.  
**Counter:** State who holds the burden and what would count as evidence. A claim does not become true by surviving an audit it never had to pass.  

1. Demanding that others disprove a claim instead of supporting it, so that the absence of disproof is treated as support.
2. It works because a demand sounds like an invitation to debate. The audience rarely notices that the person making the claim has not made a case for it.

* **Example:** *"Show me the study that says it is harmful."* The claim was that it is safe; the absent study is not a finding.
* **Easily confused with:** *Argument from ignorance* - Ignorance treats a gap as proof; the shift assigns the work of closing the gap to the sceptic.
* **Related Terms:** [*Sealioning*](#60-sealioning), [*Kafka trap*](#61-kafka-trap)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Burden of proof (philosophy)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burden_of_proof_(philosophy))

### 154. Gish gallop
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Named after Duane Gish, who debated by assertion density: more claims per minute than anyone could answer.*

**Family:** Informal · Relevance  
**Committed when:** A format with fixed time and a live audience.  
**The tell:** The reply is dozens of claims and no thread; the answer addresses four and looks evasive about the rest.  
**Counter:** Answer one claim at length and say the rest are unanswered rather than answered. Quantity was the tactic; refusing it is the counter.  

1. Overwhelming an opponent with a flood of dubious claims so that answering all of them is impossible in the time available.
2. It works on arithmetic, not argument. Each claim takes ten seconds to make and ten minutes to check, and the format does not care that they are not equally cheap.

* **Example:** *"Fourteen points: the vote, the machines, the ballots, the envelopes, the couriers…"* Fourteen claims, ninety seconds, one of them true.
* **Easily confused with:** *Ad nauseam* - Ad nauseam repeats one claim; the gallop produces a new one every sentence.
* **Related Terms:** [*Proof by assertion*](#24-proof-by-assertion), [*Shifting the burden of proof*](#153-shifting-the-burden-of-proof)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Gish gallop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gish_gallop)

### 155. Appeal to the stone
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Latin: argumentum ad lapidem - Dr Johnson refuted Berkeley by kicking a stone and saying "I refute it thus".*

**Family:** Informal · Relevance  
**Committed when:** A claim sounds wrong on its face and the speaker cannot say why.  
**The tell:** The reply is a verdict - ridiculous, obviously false, come on - with no reason attached to it.  
**Counter:** Ask for the reason. If the claim really is absurd, the reason is easy to give, and giving it is the whole difference.  

1. Dismissing a claim as absurd without saying what is wrong with it, treating obviousness as an argument.
2. It works because most absurd-sounding claims are absurd, so the reflex is usually right. The problem is that it cannot tell the two cases apart, and it never has to show its work.

* **Example:** *"That is the dumbest thing I have heard all week."* Possible. The sentence has not said which part is wrong.
* **Easily confused with:** *Appeal to ridicule* - Ridicule makes the claim funny; the stone simply declares it absurd. The stone is lazier and more common.
* **Related Terms:** [*Proof by assertion*](#24-proof-by-assertion), [*Thought-terminating cliché*](#151-thought-terminating-cliche)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Appeal to the stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_the_stone)

### 156. Shifting baseline
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Named in fisheries science: each generation measures the catch against what it remembers, not against what the water held before anyone was counting.*

**Family:** Informal · Statistical  
**Committed when:** A comparison is being made, and the reference point has quietly moved to whatever the speaker grew up with.  
**The tell:** The standard of normal is the speaker's own first memory, offered as though it were the original condition.  
**Counter:** Ask what the number was before anyone now living was measuring it. A baseline is a record, not a memory.  

1. Judging the present against the last generation's memory instead of the record: the lake still has fish, the river still runs clear enough, the airport still works, so nothing has changed.
2. It works because every generation is honest and every generation starts late. Nobody alive has seen the earlier state, so the degraded one is the only one anyone can compare against - and the comparison comes out clean every time.

* **Example:** *"The lake still has fish in it"* - it has a third of the fish it had when the speaker's father fished it, and the father's father said the same thing about the same lake.
* **Easily confused with:** *Creeping normality* - Creeping normality is the drift itself, one increment at a time; shifting baseline is why the drift goes unmeasured - each generation resets the ruler.
* **Related Terms:** [*Normalcy bias*](#158-normalcy-bias), [*Sunk cost fallacy*](#25-sunk-cost-fallacy)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Shifting baseline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shifting_baseline)

### 157. Creeping normality
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Jared Diamond's term for change that arrives in increments too small to object to, each one accepted because the last one was.*

**Family:** Informal · Presumption  
**Committed when:** The next step is being justified by the size of the step rather than the direction of the walk.  
**The tell:** Every individual change is small, defensible and already done; only the sequence is indefensible.  
**Counter:** Ask what the total is, not what the increment is. A series can be refused where a step cannot.  

1. Accepting each small change because it is small, so that a large one gets assembled out of pieces nobody had to defend: the fee, then the surcharge, then the surcharge with the waiting period.
2. It works because objection has a fixed cost and every increment is smaller than that cost. The room is never wrong about any single step; it is only never asked about the direction.

* **Example:** *"Nobody voted to end public comment."* The period went from thirty days to ten, then to "as practicable", then to a form that closed on Fridays.
* **Easily confused with:** *Slippery slope* - The slippery slope is a prediction made by someone arguing against the first step; creeping normality is the same walk recorded afterwards by someone who did not notice it.
* **Related Terms:** [*Shifting baseline*](#156-shifting-baseline), [*Normalcy bias*](#158-normalcy-bias)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Creeping normality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creeping_normality)

---

### 158. Normalcy bias
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Named for the normality it assumes: the disaster has not arrived, so the room reads the quiet as a guarantee.*

**Family:** Informal · Presumption  
**Committed when:** A warning arrives, and the first thing measured is whether anything like it has happened here before.  
**The tell:** The plan is built for the emergency that already happened, and the new one is treated as a rumour.  
**Counter:** Ask what the warning would have to look like to be believed. If no evidence could move it, the calm is the belief and not the evidence.  

1. Reading an absence of disaster as evidence against the disaster: the levee has held, the audit passed, the fever broke, so the thing being described cannot be coming.
2. It works because the quiet is real and the record is short. A hazard that has not arrived looks exactly like a hazard that is not there, and the difference only shows up once - which is the once the belief was protecting against.

* **Example:** *"We have never had a fire in this building"* - said on the fourth floor of a building with one stairwell, above the floor where the alarm panel had been disabled to stop the nuisance trips.
* **Easily confused with:** *Argument from silence* - The argument from silence reads a missing record as a settled question; normalcy bias reads a quiet record as a safe one.
* **Related Terms:** [*Shifting baseline*](#156-shifting-baseline), [*Dramagogue*](#97-dramagogue)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Normalcy bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalcy_bias)

### 159. Just-world hypothesis
*(noun)* - *fallacy*  
*Melvin Lerner's term for the belief that people get what they deserve, which turns an unexplained suffering into an accusation.*

**Family:** Informal · Improper premise  
**Committed when:** Something terrible has happened to someone, and the first question asked is what they did.  
**The tell:** The victim's conduct is examined for a cause, and the cause is found before the facts are in.  
**Counter:** Ask what this person did that the survivors did not. The question is not whether suffering is deserved but whether the explanation arrived before the evidence.  

1. Explaining suffering by the character of the sufferer: the flood victim who should not have lived there, the patient who should have gone sooner, the country that should have governed itself better.
2. It works because a deserved outcome is a bearable one. If the world pays out what people earn, then the room is safe and the sufferer is owed nothing - which is why the explanation is produced before the facts arrive, and defended after they do.

* **Example:** *"She should have left."* The sentence turns a crime into a decision, and the room into a place where it could not have happened to them.
* **Easily confused with:** *Appeal to consequences* - The appeal to consequences rejects a claim because believing it would be bad; the just-world hypothesis accepts an outcome because an undeserved one would be unbearable.
* **Related Terms:** [*Normalcy bias*](#158-normalcy-bias), [*Plutomartyr*](#101-plutomartyr)
* **Source:** [Wikipedia · Just-world hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just-world_hypothesis)

### 160. The clerk
*(noun)* `/klɑːk/` - *keystone*  
*Old English clerc, a cleric - the literate man who kept the book because he was the one who could read.*

**Office:** The minutes, the roll, and the file that proves both.  
**Call:** "That's not in the record."  
**Tell:** Reads the motion aloud, slowly · Knows the quorum rule without looking it up · Keeps the version that was actually adopted  
**Keystone:** Without the clerk there is no record, and without a record there is no way to prove what the body did - which is the first thing a Sabotriot needs and the last thing they check.  

1. The officer who keeps the body's memory: the agenda, the roll call, the minutes, the motions as adopted rather than as proposed. In most places this is an elected or appointed post with a statutory duty to the record.
2. It works because procedure is evidence. A vote nobody recorded is a vote somebody can deny, so the clerk's real power is not the gavel but the file - and the job stays invisible precisely until it is done badly.

* **Example:** *"Two years later the only proof of what the council had voted for was the **clerk**'s minutes, and the member who had denied it was reading them aloud."*
* **Easily confused with:** *The archivist* - The clerk keeps what the body did this year; the archivist keeps what it did in 1974.
* **Counters:** [*Sabotriot*](#5-sabotriot), [*Omnibust*](#10-omnibust), [*Pocketveto*](#12-pocketveto), [*Recessure*](#78-recessure), [*Discharge petition*](#17-discharge-petition)

### 161. The poll worker
*(noun)* `/pəʊl ˈwɜː.kə/` - *keystone*  
*English: the precinct official who works the poll - a temporary, trained, paid job anyone can take.*

**Office:** The roll, the provisional ballot, and the chain of custody that starts when the box is sealed.  
**Call:** "Name and address, please."  
**Tell:** Reads the instruction card out loud · Offers the provisional ballot before the argument starts · Knows the closing time and the recount rule  
**Keystone:** Without them the ballot is a rumour and the count is an assertion. The Critizen needs an empty table to make 'That doesn't apply to me' sound like law; the poll worker is the person who fills it.  

1. The precinct official who checks the roll, issues the ballot, and signs the paperwork that lets the count be reconstructed afterwards. The job is temporary and public, which is the point: the count is run by neighbours who can be watched.
2. It works because every ballot that crosses the table is accounted for by two people who are allowed to disagree. The whole apparatus exists so that a stranger with a clipboard can prove the number is right, months later, without trusting anybody.

* **Example:** *"The scanner jammed at nine, and the **poll worker** did what the training had covered: sealed the bin, called the county, and read the numbers into the log with an observer watching."*
* **Easily confused with:** *The clerk* - The clerk records what the body did; the poll worker records what the voter did, one name at a time.

### 162. The archivist
*(noun)* `/ˈɑː.kɪ.vɪst/` - *keystone*  
*From the Greek arkheion, the house of the magistrate - the building where the record was kept because it was the record.*

**Office:** The retention schedule, the box number, and the thing everybody assumed was saved.  
**Call:** "We have that. It's in the 1974 series."  
**Tell:** Asks what you intend to do with it · Refuses to let a file leave the building · Can date a photograph by the paper  
**Keystone:** Without the archive the past is a rumour with a monument, and every Mythstorian gets to write the decade. Retention schedules are boring until the only copy of the decision is in a shredder.  

1. The keeper of the record after the news cycle: the box, the series, and the schedule that says what may be destroyed and what may not. Archivists are usually the only people in an institution with a professional duty to keep things nobody is asking for.
2. It works because provenance is checkable. A document with a series, a date and a custodian can be authenticated by a stranger; a document in somebody's drawer cannot - and that difference decides whether a claim about the past can be settled at all.

* **Example:** *"The department said the policy had never existed, and the **archivist** produced the 1998 memo, the routing slip, and the name of the official who had signed it."*
* **Easily confused with:** *The librarian* - The archivist keeps the thing; the librarian finds it.
* **Counters:** [*Mythstorian*](#69-mythstorian), [*Friday news dump*](#42-friday-news-dump), [*Pseudo-event*](#115-pseudo-event), [*Hypocontortionist*](#68-hypocontortionist), [*Secret meeting*](#87-secret-meeting)

### 163. The librarian
*(noun)* `/laɪˈbreə.ri.ən/` - *keystone*  
*From the Latin librarius, a copyist - the trade that became a profession the day the public was allowed in.*

**Office:** The reference desk, the database licence, and the right to ask a question that sounds stupid.  
**Call:** "Let's find out together."  
**Tell:** Answers with a source and a call number · Says 'I don't know' without flinching · Teaches the search instead of the answer  
**Keystone:** Without the desk, 'do your own research' means 'go alone into the feed', which is the Illogician's entire business model. The library is the one place in town where being wrong is free.  

1. The professional who finds things: the reference interview, the interlibrary loan, the database the public paid for and cannot afford alone. The job is not to know the answer; it is to know how the answer is checked.
2. It works because the desk is neutral by design. A librarian has no stake in which source wins, which is why the same question can be asked there without a cost - and why the institution gets defunded by people who would rather the question cost something.

* **Example:** *"He had done his own research for a year, and the **librarian** found the study he had been citing, the correction to it, and the retraction, in about eleven minutes."*
* **Easily confused with:** *The editor* - The librarian finds the source; the editor refuses to publish without it.

### 164. The caseworker
*(noun)* `/ˈkeɪsˌwɜː.kə/` - *keystone*  
*English: the official who carries a caseload - the point where a statute becomes a decision about one household.*

**Office:** The form, the deadline, and the discretion to read a rule the way it was written.  
**Call:** "You qualify. Here's the page that says so."  
**Tell:** Knows the appeal deadline better than the appellant · Writes the reason into the letter · Answers the phone on the second ring  
**Keystone:** Without the caseworker, policy is a press release. The Plutomartyr needs a benefit that arrives as a favour rather than as an entitlement; the caseworker is the person who hands it over as a right.  

1. The front-line official who turns a statute into a decision about one person: the form, the means test, the letter that says yes or no. Political scientists call this street-level bureaucracy, and it is where most of the state is actually experienced.
2. It works because discretion is unavoidable and therefore visible. No rule covers every case, so the caseworker decides, and the decision is only legitimate if it comes with reasons - which makes the job both the most humane part of the state and the easiest part to cut.

* **Example:** *"The letter said no, the **caseworker** read the appeal, found the clause the automated screen had missed, and the cheque arrived nine days later."*
* **Easily confused with:** *The clerk* - The clerk records the decision; the caseworker delivers it to somebody who needs it by Friday.

### 165. The editor
*(noun)* `/ˈed.ɪ.tə/` - *keystone*  
*From the Latin edere, to give out - the one who decides what leaves the building.*

**Office:** The second source, the corrections policy, and the authority to hold a story for a day.  
**Call:** "Who else has this?"  
**Tell:** Kills a story that would have worked · Puts the correction at the top, not the bottom · Answers the phone when the subject calls  
**Keystone:** Without the desk, volume is the only quality signal left and the Slopagandist wins by publishing first. Editing is the cost that makes publishing expensive enough to mean something.  

1. The person between the reporter and the press: the one who asks for the second source, reads the letter from the subject's lawyer, and decides what the story is actually claiming. The job is institutional rather than personal - it exists so the decision is not made by the person who wrote the sentence.
2. It works because a published correction is expensive. An editor who has had to print one remembers it, and that memory is the only thing standing between a newsroom and a feed - which is why the desk is the first thing cut when the business model moves to volume.

* **Example:** *"The **editor** held the story a day, the second call changed the second paragraph, and the version that ran was the one that survived being read to the person it was about."*
* **Easily confused with:** *The moderator* - The editor decides what is published; the moderator decides what gets answered.
* **Counters:** [*Slopagandist*](#102-slopagandist), [*Bluecheck*](#105-bluecheck)

### 166. The moderator
*(noun)* `/ˈmɒd.ə.reɪ.tə/` - *keystone*  
*From the Latin moderari, to keep within bounds - the same root as moderate, and the same job.*

**Office:** The question, the clock, and the rule that an answer has to be an answer.  
**Call:** "That's not what I asked."  
**Tell:** Repeats the question, word for word · Gives the same time to the side that is losing · Checks the claim while it is still in the room  
**Keystone:** Without the moderator, a debate is two broadcasts sharing a room and the Sophistroll wins by making the format the argument. A question that has to be answered is a question that can be marked unanswered.  

1. The person who runs the format: the question, the time, the follow-up, and the willingness to say on air that the answer did not address what was asked. Moderation is a procedural job that everybody mistakes for a personality.
2. It works because a live check is cheap. A moderator can ask for the source while the claim is still in the room, which is the one moment when verification costs the speaker something - and the reason formats without moderators are so popular with people who need a claim to travel.

* **Example:** *"The **moderator** asked the same question three times, got three answers about something else, and said so on air - which was the whole of the remedy."*
* **Easily confused with:** *The steelman* - The moderator runs the format; the steelman is what the format is for.

### 167. The auditor
*(noun)* `/ˈɔː.dɪ.tə/` - *keystone*  
*From the Latin audire, to hear - the officer who heard the accounts read aloud, because the books were once spoken.*

**Office:** The sample, the standard, and the report nobody commissioned.  
**Call:** "Show me the transaction."  
**Tell:** Publishes the methodology before the finding · Names the office, not the mood · Comes back the following year  
**Keystone:** Without the auditor the books are a story an office tells about itself, and the Cronycle is just a friend with a contract. Audit is the difference between a claim and a record.  

1. The examiner with a statutory right to the books: the inspector general, the state auditor, the outside firm working to a scope it did not negotiate with the audited. The output is not an opinion; it is a finding with a name and a date attached.
2. It works because the sample is public and repeatable. An audit that says how it chose what to look at can be checked by anyone, which is what separates it from a review - and why the first move against an auditor is always to shrink the scope.

1. The rule that a person with a financial interest, a family tie, or a prior role in the matter steps out of it - and says so in writing, so the step is checkable rather than gallant.
2. It works because influence is cheapest when it is invisible. A gift, a friendship or a former employer does not have to change a decision in order to change how the decision looks; recusal removes the question instead of answering it, which is the point.

* **Example:** *"The **recusal** was filed on a Friday, the substitute read the record cold, and the contract drafted for one signature went out to tender."*

### 185. Cross-examination
*(noun)* `/ˌkrɒs ɪɡˌzæm.ɪˈneɪ.ʃən/` - *remedy*  
*English legal usage: the questioning of a witness by the side that did not call them - the only part of a hearing designed to find out whether the story survives contact.*

**Effect:** Makes the claim answerable, under oath, to somebody paid to test it - and puts the answers in a transcript.  
**Costs:** A deposition, a transcript, and the risk that your own witness fares worse under the same treatment.  
**Failure mode:** The objection sustained, and the time limit that arrives before the point does.  
**Tell:** A question the witness cannot dodge · A transcript of the answer · A claim that changes under pressure  

1. The questioning of a witness by the opposing side: leading questions, documents put in front of them, and the requirement that an answer be given rather than a theme restated.
2. It works because it is the one procedure that makes a claim cost something at the moment it is made. A statement in a hearing can be a performance; the same statement under cross-examination has to survive being interrupted, dated, and read back.

### 186. The stay
*(noun)* `/ðə steɪ/` - *remedy*  
*From the Old French ester, to stand - the order that makes a thing stand still while it is argued about.*

**Effect:** Stops the thing from happening while it is being argued about, and puts the status quo back where it was.  
**Costs:** A motion, a bond, and a judge willing to be the one who halted it.  
**Failure mode:** The stay that expires quietly, and the harm ruled not irreparable because it has already happened.  
**Tell:** An order with a date on it · A bond that makes delay expensive · A status quo that actually holds  

1. The order that suspends an action while a challenge is heard: a rule, an execution, a demolition, a transfer. It is the procedural equivalent of a hand on the arm, granted or refused by a judge who has to say why.
2. It works because it changes who bears the risk of the delay. Without a stay the challenger loses by default - the thing is done before the argument is heard - so the remedy is not the ruling, it is the preservation of the possibility of one.

* **Example:** *"The **stay** came through at four in the afternoon, the demolition crew stood down, and the hearing that followed was about the building instead of about the rubble."*

**Office:** The map, the public hearing, and the requirement that the lines be drawn where the public can see them drawn.  
**Call:** "Show us the map, and the criteria you drew it with."  
**Tell:** Draws before it knows who benefits · Holds the hearing in the town the first draft splits · Publishes the criteria before the lines  
**Keystone:** Without the commission the map is drawn by the people it measures, and Gerrythm is not a flaw in the drawing - it is the drawing. Independence here is not a better class of person; it is a different address for the pencil.  

1. The body that draws electoral districts outside the legislature: a fixed membership, published criteria, draft maps released for comment, and a final map that has to be defended against the criteria rather than against the seat count.

* **Example:** *"The **redistricting commission** published three drafts, took comment in the one town the first draft split in half, and the final map moved four precincts - which was enough."*

### 188. The carve-out
*(noun)* `/ðə ˈkɑːv.aʊt/` - *remedy*  
*Legislative drafting: the exception cut into a general rule so that one category of case falls outside it.*

**Effect:** Voids the silence clause for a defined category of claim, and makes the agreement unenforceable in that respect.  
**Costs:** A legislature willing to name the category out loud, and a plaintiff willing to be the first to test it.  
**Failure mode:** The carve-out that covers the claim but not the arbitration clause that swallows it, and the category drawn so narrowly that almost nothing falls inside.  
**Tell:** A statute that names the claim · A clause struck rather than read down · A plaintiff who can afford to be the test case  

1. A statutory exception that removes a contract term's power over one kind of dispute: the silence clause that cannot be enforced in a harassment claim, the release that cannot waive a wage claim, the arbitration agreement that does not reach a civil-rights suit.
2. It works because it changes the default without banning the contract. Nobody has to prove the NDA was coerced; the clause simply has no effect where the statute says so - which is why the drafting fight moves to the definition of the category, where it is quiet and decisive.

* **Example:** *"The **carve-out** passed with a definition of harassment that excluded contractors, and the clause held for everyone the statute had not named."*
* **Easily confused with:** *NDA* - The NDA buys silence with a contract; the carve-out voids the contract for one category of claim.

### 189. Notice and comment
*(noun)* `/ˈnəʊ.tɪs ənd ˈkɒm.ent/` - *remedy*  
*American administrative law: the requirement that a proposed rule be published, opened to public comment, and answered before it binds anybody.*

**Effect:** Publishes a proposed rule, opens a docket, and requires the agency to answer the comments it received before the rule can take effect.  
**Costs:** Sixty days, a docket to read, and a comment that engages with the text rather than the mood.  
**Failure mode:** The comment period that is technically open, and the final rule identical to the draft with the comments answered in a paragraph.  
**Tell:** A docket number · A response to comments · A difference between the proposed and the final text  

1. The rulemaking procedure: publish the draft, take comment for a fixed period, answer what was said, then issue the rule. It is the administrative state's version of a hearing, and it governs thousands of decisions a year that no camera ever covers.
2. It works because it creates a record before the decision instead of after it. A comment that names a flaw in the text has to be answered on the record, and an answer on the record is the thing a court can review - which is why the procedure is worth more than the consultation it resembles.

* **Example:** *"The **notice and comment** docket drew nine thousand comments, four of them from people who had read the appendix, and the final rule moved the threshold they had questioned."*
* **Easily confused with:** *School board meeting* - The meeting takes comment in a room; notice and comment takes it in a docket that outlives the room.

### 190. The withdrawal clause
*(noun)* `/ˈwɪð.drɔː.əl klɔːz/` - *remedy*  
*English: the paragraph at the back of an agreement that decides how a signatory may leave it - notice, survival, and price.*

**Effect:** Turns an exit into a scheduled act with a bill attached: notice in writing, a published list of the obligations that survive the withdrawal, and a successor agreement that names what leaving costs.  
**Costs:** Negotiating leverage, because a state that prices its own exit has admitted it might use it.  
**Failure mode:** The clause that sets a notice period and nothing else - twelve months of waiting, then the same exit with a cleaner headline.  
**Tell:** A notice period with a date on it · A list of obligations that survive the signature · Allies who price the exit into the next agreement  

1. The clause that makes withdrawal a procedure rather than an announcement: a period of notice, a statement of what survives the exit, and a schedule of what the departing party still owes.
2. It works because the benefit of an agreement is paid over decades and the cost of leaving is paid once, in a news cycle. Moving the cost into the text - a notice period the market can read, obligations that outlive the signature - makes the arbitrage visible while it is being proposed instead of after it is done.

* **Example:** *"The **withdrawal clause** bought the other parties a year of notice and a list of agreements that would outlive the government that left; the announcement ran three minutes and the bill ran four years."*
* **Easily confused with:** *Sunset clause* - The sunset clause gives a power a date; the withdrawal clause gives an exit a price, and publishes it before anyone leaves.

**Tell:** A funder named in the ad, not after it · A list published before the ballot · A shell company that has to name its own shell

### 192. The reserve
*(noun)* `/ðə rɪˈzɜːv/` - *remedy*  
*French réserve, from Latin reservare, to keep back: the stockpile held against the day a price becomes a policy.*

**Effect:** Breaks the price lever by holding the thing in stock: barrels released on a published schedule, substitutes bought before they are needed, and contracts that outlast the spot price.  
**Costs:** Money parked in a warehouse, and the political price of spending it at the moment it is most useful.  
**Failure mode:** The release that arrives after the price has already moved, and the reserve that becomes the market's new floor.  
**Tell:** A release schedule with dates on it · A stock level published monthly · A contract whose term is longer than the price cycle  

1. The strategic stockpile as a counter to supply leverage: a government that can put barrels, gas, or grain on the market on a schedule has made the commodity a worse weapon.
2. It works because leverage needs scarcity to stay credible. A published reserve turns a threat into a calculation - the squeeze has to outlast the stockpile, and a stockpile is measured in months while an election is measured in weeks.

* **Example:** *"**The reserve** released nine million barrels over six weeks, and the foreign minister's visit was rescheduled for the following quarter."*
* **Easily confused with:** *Sunset clause* - The sunset clause puts a date on a power; the reserve puts a quantity between the power and its price.
* **Counters:** [*Oil diplomacy*](#122-oil-diplomacy)
* **Related Terms:** [*Oil diplomacy*](#122-oil-diplomacy), [*The statistician*](#183-the-statistician), [*Forever war*](#124-forever-war)
